### Cancer as a voltage failure
- **id:** cancer-voltage
- **pillar:** support
- **summary:** Kruse says any lasting fall in the voltage of the mitochondria's inner membrane can produce cancer, a protective retreat and an electrical short circuit that the environment, not drugs, must reverse.
- **protocols:** cancer-glioma

Kruse sees cancer as an electrical failure rather than mainly a genetic one. In his model the cell's power plants, the mitochondria, hold a strong charge across their inner membrane, and anything that lets that voltage sag for long enough can tip a cell into cancer; he even names the fields around wind turbines, given years of exposure. The tumor, to him, is a retreat the cell makes to survive, so the fix lies in restoring the environment rather than in drugs.

#### How a cell falls into cancer

Kruse describes a healthy cell at about −70 millivolts and a failing one at −15 to −30. At that lower level, he says, a gate in the inner membrane (UCP-2) loses the voltage that keeps it sealed. Water rich in deuterium, a heavy form of hydrogen with an extra neutron, then floods the mitochondria's interior.

In his account deuterium's extra mass brakes ATP synthase, the spinning motor that makes the cell's energy currency, ATP. Complex IV, the last step of the energy chain, then stops making the cell's own clean, low-deuterium water. Without that water the field across the inner membrane short-circuits.

He traces the slide one step further back as well. NAD+, the carrier that ferries electrons into the energy chain, can pick up deuterium in place of light hydrogen, and once that buffer is lost oxygen turns reactive, the membrane charge drops, and deuterium floods first the cell's fluid and then the mitochondria's interior. The share of damaged mitochondrial DNA in the cell (its heteroplasmy) climbs as this happens.

The cell then falls back on fermentation, the ancient oxygen-free way of burning sugar, and begins dividing out of control. This switch is called the Warburg shift, after Otto Warburg, who saw cancer as damaged respiration rather than mutation; Kruse says his voltage model completes Warburg's picture, and he calls cancer an atavism, a throwback to an older way of living, rather than a disease.

#### The "matrix fire" and its causes

Kruse calls the stalled interior a "matrix fire." Oxygen that can't be used cleanly flips into singlet oxygen, a reactive form that attacks membranes and DNA, and the faint light cells naturally give off (ultra-weak photon emission) turns into chaotic noise. The growth pathways cancer drugs target (PI3K, Akt, mTOR) are, in his reading, readouts of that fire, not its cause.

He names four things that uncouple Complex IV: missing near-infrared light; nnEMF (non-native electromagnetic fields from wireless devices and wiring); deuterium; and the wrong copper isotope, since he says Complex IV is built to run on heavy copper-65. Like weight gain, he suggests, cancer can be a "safe mode": a lower-energy state that staves off sudden organ failure and buys time, which can be won back once the interference is removed.

#### A body-wide electrical circuit

Kruse draws support from Björn Nordenström, a radiologist at Sweden's Karolinska Institute who built on Robert O. Becker's work. Nordenström described a second circulation in the body, an electrical one running through blood vessels and the fluid between cells, which he called "biologically closed electric circuits." Between 1978 and 1983 he treated lung and breast tumors by inserting electrodes and passing a weak direct current, which drew white blood cells to the tumor and shut down its blood supply.

Chinese doctors later used this "electrochemical therapy" on more than 10,000 patients, Kruse notes, reporting success above 70% in some solid tumors, while Western medicine looked away. He tells a similar story about Georges Lakhovsky, who in 1925 at the Salpêtrière hospital in Paris infected geraniums with a tumor-causing bacterium; the one plant circled by an open copper ring shed its tumor while the rest died. Work like this was buried, he says, because a copper ring is free and chemotherapy is not. To him it confirms cancer as a bioelectric short circuit.

#### Magnetism, the brain and the night

Magnetism sits at the root of his model. Kruse says oxygen keeps its safe, stable form (triplet oxygen) only when a person is "pinned": grounded, sunlit and living under a stable local magnetic field. His shortest chain for glioma, a brain tumor, is no pinning, then singlet oxygen, then glioma. Glioblastoma, he argues, starts when foreign magnetic fields break the junctions that link mitochondria into one network, and restoring that connection can extend survival beyond what doctors expect.

Because Earth's field is weakening, he expects a magnetic excursion (a large, rapid drop and shift in the field) to drive many cancers. Without spin pinning, chromosomes misalign as cells divide, and the mutations oncology tracks are debris left by that failure.

He reads Australia's melanoma epidemic as an early example. It began on the west of the continent and has spread to the Sydney coast, which he ties to the South Atlantic Anomaly (SAA), a vast and growing weak patch in Earth's field now reaching into the Indian Ocean. The skin's layered network of small arteries works, in his model, as a "distal refinery" that sheds heat and deuterium through cold and sweat. Once those vessels lose their melanin, the light-absorbing pigment, they can no longer shield the skin's mitochondria from the combined load of light and nnEMF, and melanoma follows.

Night is part of the picture. Citing studies from Russel Reiter's group, Kruse says cancer cells drift back toward normal oxygen-burning at night, when mitochondria release melatonin, and return to fermentation by day. He calls this nightly repair "magnetic chemotherapy," and says light at night switches it off.

#### In practice

His priorities for cancer and glioma: get magnetically pinned first; ground; take natural UV-A and infrared sunlight and don't fear UV-B; remove nnEMF; and for glioma, sleep in a cold bedroom. At high latitudes, he adds, naturally low-deuterium water won't protect someone who lives indoors under artificial light. He believes UV-A light, life on a zero-declination line and tree resins such as mastic gum, which he says are naturally low in deuterium, can help turn cancer cells back from fermentation toward normal respiration. He calls a ketogenic diet unwise as a cancer strategy because many aggressive tumors (prostate, glioblastoma, triple-negative breast) burn fat too, so cutting glucose alone can select fat-burning clones. Nor does he see drugs as the main answer, pointing to a 15-year Oxford trial in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in which surgery or radiation for localized prostate cancer did not lengthen survival compared with monitoring.

Kruse treats diabetes as a problem of light, deuterium and magnetism, not of food. #### Insulin resistance as a circuit breaker

Kruse calls insulin resistance a protective shield, not a defect: a circuit breaker that trips when a cell is offered more fuel than it can safely burn. Indoor spaces, he says, are dominated by blue light and magnetically "dead," and running the cell's energy chain under that light upsets the electrons' spin, which drives insulin spikes. He puts high blood sugar and insulin down mainly to light and to a lack of "pinning" (being grounded under a stable local field), and says meal timing under LEDs drives type 2 diabetes more than food does.

In a low-field room full of nnEMF (wireless and wiring fields), he says, burning glucose is a "dirty burn" that throws off flares of the faint light cells emit and leaves deuterium behind. Extra fuel would stall ATP synthase, the cell's energy motor, and could tip the cell toward cancer, so refusing it is self-protection. Forcing glucose in with injected insulin is, in his phrase, a "thermodynamic crime," like pushing more current through a transformer that is already shorting; he says it damages DNA and the melanin lining the blood vessels.

The shield works, in his account, through the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle, a back-door route that carries hydrogen into the mitochondria. He says it runs only during insulin resistance and favors light hydrogen, so deuterium is left behind in the cell fluid instead of jamming the energy motor. He points to animals that use this on purpose: the bar-tailed godwit flying 11,000 km without food, and the naked mole-rat surviving without oxygen. High-dose insulin, by contrast, forces deuterium in; he calls it a "biophysical degausser" because it strips cell membranes of their charge.

The godwit, he adds, makes the flight with high blood sugar and full insulin resistance, burning its own fat and gut, yet performs at its peak. What protects it is the pecten oculi, a comb of blood vessels packed with melanin inside the bird's eye, which he calls a "photonic heat sink": it turns the metabolic noise of the flight into usable current and shields the bird's magnetic compass. Humans do the same, in his account, with the choroid plexus (the tissue that makes spinal fluid) and the retina's pigment layer. High sugar is survivable when that melanin shielding is intact, and diabetics, he says, have stripped theirs with nnEMF and blue light. Research on night-active mice misses this, because day-living birds and humans share a melanin-led clock.

Fat burning gives the cell another way to set sugar aside. Burning saturated fat, Kruse says, pushes electrons backward through Complex I, the energy chain's entry point, and an iron-sulfur sensor there reads this reverse electron flow as a signal to skip glucose and fructose. That is why, to him, high blood sugar in the cold can be healthy.

#### The beta cell's jammed motor

In type 2, Kruse says, the beta cell (the pancreas cell that makes insulin) is usually full of insulin but can't release it. Release needs a burst of energy from ATP synthase, the cell's rotary motor. Table sugar and corn syrup, which he says plants load with deuterium, eaten under blue light without balancing red light, break up the cell's structured water and let deuterium jam that motor. "Beta-cell fatigue" is therefore, to him, an isotope jam; left long enough, singlet oxygen (a reactive form of oxygen) damages the cell until the body removes it.

The pancreas normally clears deuterium through what he calls the vagal "tailpipe." The vagus nerve drives an enzyme, carbonic anhydrase, to make bicarbonate, which binds deuterium and carries it out through the pancreatic ducts into the gut. Sugar under blue light, or a magnetic excursion, knocks the enzyme out of alignment and "welds the tailpipe shut," so deuterium backs up into the beta cells.

Clearing that exhaust, Kruse argues, lets the cell resolve its own diabetes whatever the carbohydrate intake. Only once the flush runs, shown by a well-formed (Bristol type 4) stool, can beta cells make insulin again, so the key is clearing deuterium from the vagal nerves that serve them. He says sunlight lowers blood glucose by about 27%, that advanced type 2 patients have regained insulin secretion with deuterium depletion, and that beta-cell stem cells can recover through the light-driven repair Robert O. Becker described. Type 2 diabetics also lose less weight on GLP-1 drugs, he says, because their energy chain is already stalled ("Marcus-inverted," pushed past the point where electrons can flow), so the drug strips muscle rather than fat.

The morning rise in blood sugar, the "dawn phenomenon," is in his reading a failure to catch the morning magnetic shift, not a hormone surge. At daybreak the upper atmosphere ionizes and the local field changes, and healthy cells use that wave to wake. A diabetic's damaged membranes can't capture it, so voltage drops and the liver dumps glucose to keep the cell's battery alive.

India is his main example. He has tracked India's diabetes alongside Australia's melanoma for about ten years and treats them as one disease of the South Atlantic Anomaly (SAA), the vast weak patch in Earth's field, which he believes has pushed into the southern Indian Ocean and reached the southern tip of India. The damage, he says, travels through the pancreas's blood supply. Beta cells draw up to a fifth of the pancreas's blood flow from a dense capillary bed, much like the brain's, fed from the superior mesenteric artery, the main artery to the gut. A shifting field and nnEMF load that artery with deuterium and block the body's own melanin-making, "bleaching" the arterial tree even while the skin stays dark.
