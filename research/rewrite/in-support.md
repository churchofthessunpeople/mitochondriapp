### Sleep sanctuary
- **id:** sleep-space
- **pillar:** support
- **summary:** Kruse calls insomnia a light-and-magnetic hardware failure, so the bedroom must be dark, cold, grounded, wireless-free and aligned with the field.
- **protocols:** sleep-space, north-south-bed

Kruse treats insomnia as a hardware problem, not a chemical shortage: in his model the body cannot sleep well when the bedroom's light and magnetic environment is wrong. That is why he says sleeping pills and melatonin supplements make things worse, and why he puts the room itself at the center of sleep repair.

His reasoning starts with melatonin. The melatonin that matters most to him is not the small amount the pineal gland releases into the blood, but the much larger amount mitochondria (the cell's energy-producing units) make inside cells overnight. Drawing on Russel Reiter's work, he calls this nightly melatonin "magnetic chemotherapy": while you sleep, it calms damaged cells and nudges cells drifting toward cancer back toward normal behavior.

Light after dark switches that repair off. Kruse points to the white LED, built by coating a blue LED with a yellow phosphor to save energy. Its strong blue peak fires the eye's daytime sensors (light-sensitive retinal cells that tell the brain clock it is day), so a screen or LED bulb at night tells the clock it is noon, and cells skip their overnight melatonin.

Darkness alone is not enough, he argues. Making melatonin needs two kinds of "containment" around the mitochondrial membrane, one electric and one magnetic. Grounding (skin contact with the earth, or a grounded sheet) restores only the electric part. The magnetic part depends on the field around the bed, which is why Wi-Fi, wiring and electric beds can ruin sleep even in a pitch-black room. He is blunt that an electric bed is a mistake.

Cold matters for a separate reason. In deep sleep the spaces between brain cells widen and spinal fluid moves through the brain in a spiral he calls a vortex. In his model a cold room contracts brain tissue slightly, which narrows the vortex and makes it spin faster. That flings deuterium (heavy hydrogen, which he says jams the mitochondrial motor) out of the brain toward the lymphatic drains in the neck.

The last piece is direction. Kruse wants the bed on a north–south line with the head to the north, so the brain's magnetic iron crystals and the iron-containing proteins in mitochondria lie parallel to Earth's field lines all night. Because the magnetic pole is now moving quickly, he says this alignment should be checked against today's field rather than set once and forgotten.

In practice, he calls the result a "magnetic sanctuary." His sleep-space protocol asks for Wi-Fi, routers and other wireless devices switched off before sleep, and total darkness, with red incandescent light or full red blockers after sunset instead of LEDs. It also means no electric bed and no Bluetooth sleep trackers, a grounded sheet, and a cold room, ideally on the ground floor rather than high in a building. His north–south bed protocol adds the head-north alignment, rechecked as the pole moves. Where the local field is weak he suggests sleeping all night on a static magnetic pad, or, if that is unaffordable, putting a coil of heavy copper pipe under the pillow. Sleeping pills and melatonin pills stay on his avoid list.

### Sleep in his model: adenosine, myelin and the nightly flip
- **id:** myelin-sleep-battery
- **pillar:** support
- **summary:** Kruse explains sleep as the nightly recharge of an electrical grid: adenosine signals the membrane is tiring, myelin recharges as a proton battery, and the brain flips polarity to purge deuterium.
- **protocols:** sleep-space, binaural-sleep-audio

Kruse explains sleep as the nightly recharge of the body's electrical grid rather than simple rest. In his model three things happen in sequence: a chemical alarm signals that the grid is tiring, the brain's white matter recharges like a battery, and the brain reverses its electrical polarity to clear deuterium (heavy hydrogen). When any step fails, he says, sleep disorders follow.

#### The adenosine alarm

During the day, mitochondria hold a very strong electric field across their inner membrane (he gives 30 million volts per metre). In his view this field is what lets the membrane let in ordinary hydrogen and keep heavy hydrogen out. Hours of waking work create friction that slows electrons, so the voltage sags and the water inside cells drifts from its structured, charge-holding state toward ordinary "bulk" water.

To avoid a blowout, which he calls Marcus inversion (electron flow stalling once it is pushed past its best rate), the cell strips phosphate groups off its ATP fuel. The adenosine left over is his "smoke alarm": it tells the brainstem's arousal network that the grid is tiring and taking on deuterium, and that triggers sleep. He adds that an ungrounded day also builds up oleamide, a fatty signal that eventually shuts the connections between brain cells and stalls the thalamus (the brain's relay and pacing center). It is cleared overnight. For this reason he places sleep in the state of brain water and the heat of the spinal fluid, not in microtubules (tiny protein tubes inside neurons).

#### Myelin as a proton battery

Kruse hails a 2026 hypothesis by Felix Scholkmann and colleagues as support for his model. Myelin, the fatty wrapping around nerve fibers, is not passive insulation but a proton battery charged during sleep. Myelin basic protein, packed with positively charged amino acids, is the "anode" that grips protons. Proteolipid protein, which spans the membrane, is the "wire" that carries them to ATP synthase, the motor that makes ATP.

Because myelin has few mitochondria of its own, he says mitochondria ship working ATP synthase to it overnight in small membrane packages. At the same time melatonin cools the brain by 1–2 °C, which tightens the relevant enzymes so deuterium is filtered out as the battery refills.

Several conclusions follow in his model. Sleep need scales inversely with white-matter volume: bats sleep about 20 hours, humans about 8, elephants about 3.5. Sleep loss, marathons, multiple sclerosis and brain injury drain the battery, and an indoor, blue-lit life lets myelin water collapse in a way no sleeping pill can fix. He also says there is no genetic night owl. A chronic night owl, to him, is an ungrounded person whose cellular battery is so low the master clock cannot find its starting line, and environment, not clock genes, resets it.

#### The nightly polarity flip

By day, Kruse says, electrons moving across Earth's field lines create a "biological Hall voltage" that helps hold the membrane field. At sunset, melanin in the skin and brain, with the hippocampus acting as a switchboard, should reverse polarity. Following Robert O. Becker's direct-current research, he describes current then flowing outward from the body's core to its edges. This "nocturnal Hall current inversion" is his main deuterium purge engine, and in dream sleep the brainstem paralyzes the body while it runs.

Screens, Wi-Fi, jet lag across time zones, a poor local field and too little near-infrared light all stop the flip and leave the spinal fluid loaded with deuterium; grounded sleep lets melanin make the switch. He reads sleep apnea and restless legs as emergency jolts. Apnea uses an airway collapse and adrenaline surge to restart a stalled brain circuit, and restless legs use leg contractions to restart a stalled pathway in the leg arteries. Insomnia and bipolar swings, he says, reflect a broken brainstem-to-gut nerve circuit. That circuit depends on melanin, and when it is clogged with lipofuscin (his "rusted melanin," degraded pigment debris), the flip cannot start.

#### In practice

He points to his sleep-space protocol (a dark, cold, grounded, wireless-free bedroom with the bed north–south) so the battery and the flip can work. He pairs it with daytime sun that includes near-infrared, and morning sun and grounding to reset a night owl. His binaural sleep audio, a theta-to-delta tone glide while falling asleep, is meant to sync the brain's nightly fluid pulsing. For sleep, MS or white-matter problems he recommends changing the environment, including relocating, rather than taking pills.

### Cold thermogenesis and the brain's fluid vortex
- **id:** cold-thermogenesis-skin-50
- **pillar:** support
- **summary:** Kruse calls cold a "quantum vice" that compresses tissue to keep deuterium out — an override for poor sun or field, with lineage and timing caveats.
- **protocols:** cold-thermogenesis

Kruse calls cold thermogenesis (deliberate cold exposure) a "quantum vice": a squeeze that tightens tissue so deuterium (heavy hydrogen) cannot get in. It matters in his model because cold is the main override when sunlight or the local magnetic field is poor, as in winter, at high latitude or in a bad location.

#### How the vice works

When nerve tissue is chilled it contracts, and cells must reorganize their fats to keep their membranes from freezing stiff. In white matter, Kruse says, this squeeze narrows the spacing in myelin's proteins so they clamp onto protons, and narrows the entry channels so the deuteron (deuterium's nucleus, about twice the mass of ordinary hydrogen) no longer fits. The result in his model is cell water restored to its structured, charge-holding state, which he scores as "160" against 78 for ordinary water, even when your location is poor.

He adds a point about phase change. Heavy water freezes at 3.8 °C rather than 0 °C and changes state differently from ordinary water, so a sharp cold shock at the skin surface changes local water conditions in a way he says helps shake deuterium loose.

#### Cold and dopamine

Kruse leans on Pavel Šrámek's study from Charles University in Prague, in which ten young men sat neck-deep for an hour in water at 32, 20 and 14 °C. At 14 °C, norepinephrine rose about 530% and dopamine about 250%, which he contrasts with roughly 200% from Adderall. He stresses that the dopamine stayed raised for two to three hours and drifted back without dipping below baseline, so there is no crash, tolerance or addiction. He reads ADHD as deuterium loading in the brain's hypothalamic and limbic regions and says cold works better than stimulants. The daily dose he draws from this is short: 60–180 seconds on bare skin, cold enough to make you want out, ideally in the morning.

#### Where cold fits, and its limits

In his hierarchy, cold is the override for winter and poor locations and should come before any diet change. Used as the only fix in a place like California, though, he calls it unsustainable. Cold and sauna can work against each other, he says, but only for someone who doesn't understand why to use each.

He sets limits. For East and Southeast Asian maternal lineages (haplogroups D5b1 and F) in a weak field, water at 55–65 °F can "freeze" the energy engine, because the short-lived electron pairs the cell relies on need a strong field. Those people should first maximize morning UV and infrared light, ground fully and shield from wireless fields. Timing matters too. At dawn, neck-deep water absorbs the infrared part of sunlight, scrambles the light's angle and acts like a Faraday cage, so he prefers feet in the surf with the rest of the bare body in the air. In the tropics, natural water is always cooler than the body, so ordinary swimming counts as cold therapy.

#### Targeted cold and the head's fluid vortex

Kruse also uses cold on specific spots. Ice on the roof of the mouth, under the chin or on the back of the neck contracts tissue and blood vessels and narrows what he calls the cranial "vortex chamber," so spinal fluid spins at a sharper angle and faster. Deuterium, being heavier, cannot hold the tight orbit and is thrown out to the salivary and lymphatic drains. Sleeping in a cold room does the same overnight. He also says cold packs the inner mitochondrial membrane more tightly, so its first energy-extracting complex strips electrons from food faster, and he uses groin cold in his erectile-dysfunction advice.

#### In practice

His cold-thermogenesis protocol is a daily 60–180 seconds of cold on bare skin, ideally in the morning, leaning on cold more heavily in winter or a poor location. At sunrise he wants feet in the surf rather than full immersion, and in the tropics he counts natural-water swimming. He adds targeted ice on the palate, under the chin or on the back of the neck, and a cold bedroom. People from D5b1 or F lineages living in a weak field should hold off on deep cold until they are well grounded and sunlit.

### Movement, and when exercise becomes a toxin
- **id:** morning-movement
- **pillar:** support
- **summary:** Kruse supports daily outdoor movement but says exercise turns harmful when the body is deuterium-loaded or unpinned, so environment comes before fitness.
- **protocols:** exercise

Kruse supports daily movement, but argues that exercise only helps a body whose environment is already right. In a body loaded with deuterium (heavy hydrogen) or "unpinned" (not anchored by a stable magnetic field, grounding and sunlight), he says exercise becomes a toxin. His rule is environment first, fitness second.

#### Why movement matters

In Kruse's model the real purpose of exercise is electrical. Bone and collagen are piezoelectric (they make a small current when squeezed or bent), so movement releases current that supports the spiral flow and charge of the blood. That keeps deuterium circulating and away from the mitochondria, and moving joints spin deuterium out of themselves. Mechanical and gravitational loading, together with a stable, flat local field and grounding, also keep the elastin in artery walls healthy.

He adds a pump. Citing a mouse study on the link between the abdomen and the cortex, he says abdominal muscle contractions drive the fluid flow that cleans the brain during waking hours. Astronauts lose this pump as their core weakens in microgravity. He also reads one study's gene data as showing that single workouts produce only weak, messy changes, while weeks of steady training produce an ordered shift. Long-running pulsed electric current in sea squirts produced the same shift, which in his model shows that sustained current, not effort itself, rebuilds the cell.

#### When exercise turns harmful

Kruse cites a Nature Aging study, shared by Daniel Tawfik, that defines muscle aging as an energy crisis in which the key energy-making enzymes decline. He agrees that structured training beats counting steps. But in his view exercise is a toxin when cell water is deuterium-loaded or when oxygen has flipped into its reactive "singlet" state instead of its stable "triplet" state. Deuterium slows the flow of protons, extra demand stalls electrons in the energy chain, and singlet oxygen damages membranes. He names the strength coach Charles Poliquin as someone he had warned.

He lists specific traps. Compression-and-cooling machines such as Vasper, he says, cause bursts of reperfusion damage in energy-poor muscle. Building muscle in old age is one of the costliest things a cell can do and steals energy from DNA repair, immunity and overnight brain clearance, leaving "a big frame on a bankrupt core." Watching television and then working out undoes the benefit, because screen light loads the cell with deuterium and disrupts its clock, and years of running ungrounded in rubber-soled shoes destroy joints. He uses Mark Sisson's joint replacements as his example. Blue-lit gyms are a problem when you are unwell, and flights and altitude raise the risk of muscle loss.

#### Overtraining and the brain

Overtraining harms the brain too. Kruse presents a Cell Metabolism study (Huang and colleagues) in which excess lactate in strained muscle makes it release mitochondrial "pretenders": vesicles that cross into the hippocampus, block the delivery of healthy mitochondria and occupy their docking sites at synapses. In the study's UK Biobank data, moderate exercise protected against dementia while vigorous exercise beyond a threshold sharply raised the risk. He sees the same pattern in fit people who drop dead in competition, such as a coach who collapsed at a Bangkok HYROX event. He tracks where such sudden deaths cluster by ZIP code and says it is now survival of the wisest, not the fittest.

#### In practice

His exercise protocol is daily movement outdoors, grounded and in sunlight, at moderate rather than extreme intensity. Order matters to him. First leave the wireless "soup," ground and sleep in a low-EMF bedroom, then get morning sun with its infrared, and only then add training. He would not start a sick, unpinned person on Zone 2 or interval training. He also warns against going straight from a screen to a workout, running ungrounded in rubber soles, training in blue-lit gyms when unwell, using compression machines while deuterium-loaded, and making muscle-building a goal in old age.

### The vagus nerve: deuterium drain and its stimulation

In Kruse's model the vagus nerve, the long nerve running from the brainstem to the heart, lungs and gut, is the body's main exhaust pipe for deuterium (heavy hydrogen). Together with lymph and spinal-fluid flow, it carries disorder out of the system. He calls it the body's "renormalization operator," and when its tone fails he says heavy hydrogen backs up into the brain. Much of his advice on breathing, sound and nerve stimulation is aimed at keeping this drain open.

#### The body's exhaust pipe

Kruse says the vagus supplies a steady rhythmic signal that keeps the barrier cells of the gut, lungs and blood vessels electrically charged. That charge keeps potassium in place and the water around those cells structured, which he says physically screens out deuterium. When vagal tone fades from wireless exposure, artificial blue light and lack of grounding, heavy hydrogen leaks in. It then backs up through the gut's nerve network into the brain past the fourth ventricle (the fluid chamber at the base of the brain).

He ties the vagus to digestion through what he calls the bicarbonate "tailpipe." In a strong field, the vagus drives the pancreas and duodenum to release about two liters of bicarbonate a day, and this negatively charged carrier binds deuterium and sweeps it out of the body. Sugar under blue light, or a disturbed field, "welds the tailpipe shut." He also describes a "universal" 2 Hz rhythm that keeps tissues in sync, and says immune alarm signals and deuterium jam it.

#### Left and right are not the same

Kruse gives each side of the nerve a job. The right vagus is the "cardiac operator": it damps chaotic signals to the heart so they cannot lock into racing or fibrillating rhythms. The left vagus is the "input operator": it monitors the gut and liver so their incoming deuterium load does not overwhelm the mitochondria. He links childhood nephrotic syndrome (a kidney disorder that leaks protein) to fading right-vagus control of the kidney.

That split sets his firmest rule: electrical stimulation goes on the left side only. Nearly all approved implants and ear devices (taVNS, stimulation through the skin of the ear) are restricted to the left side, he notes, because the right neck vagus sends thick fibers straight to the heart's pacemaker. Stimulating it sends the signal down to the heart instead of up to the brainstem, and can slow the heart dangerously, block its rhythm or stop it.

#### Tuning by breath, voice and sound

Before devices, Kruse ranks everyday ways to tone the vagus. At the top is slow belly breathing with long exhales, which he calls probably the most powerful repeatable vagal stimulus. Next come exercise, the cold diving reflex, mind–body practices, laughter and social contact, then humming, chanting, gargling, massage, gentle neck movement and slow nature walks. Mouth breathing works against all of it.

Sound has a special place. Neolithic chambers such as Newgrange resonate near 110 Hz, the pitch of a man's voice, and he says they were built to amplify chanting. Falling water and chanting "OM" match his 2 Hz rhythm. A 128 Hz tuning fork on the sternum in the evening, and the "Vagus Rx" in his Time Subtraction blog, round out the passive toolkit. He also says ear stimulation and chewing can shrink surfer's ear.

#### Devices and their guardrails

Kruse treats a TENS unit (a small device that sends pulsed current through skin pads) as a blunt tool, and says most commercial units are harsh enough to scramble electron spins and over-conduct through melanin. His home settings are gentle. He wants a biphasic waveform (one whose charge reverses each pulse, so nothing builds up under the pads) at 0.5–2.0 mA, below sensation or a faint tingle and never enough to twitch muscle. Pulse width should be 200–250 µs, at a steady 25 Hz, for 15–30 minutes once or twice a day. He reserves 40 Hz for brain rhythms, not the vagus.

For the vagus itself, the ear clip goes on the left tragus or inner ear fold, with the return pad on the bone just behind the same ear. A separate pad pair over the upper abdomen and the matching mid-back serves the gut's nerve plexus. Pads never go on the neck, where they can trigger the carotid pressure sensors, on the temple, or anywhere on the right. The black negative lead goes nearest the target nerve. Among commercial stimulators he rates the Neuropod best for Parkinson's and other neurodegeneration. He reports that vagal tumors (glomus tumors) shrink when stimulation is applied below them, and he would not use vagus hydrodissection (injecting fluid to free the nerve).

He also offers powerless alternatives to TENS that avoid current altogether. One is resting grounded with a smooth piece of black basalt or volcanic clay on the bone behind the ear or at the temple. Others are wearing a plain, unclosed 12-inch copper ring around the head or keeping a paramagnetic stone in the pillow. A copper ring at the neck, he says, does the same job as a vagus stimulator by replacing energy that missing melanin should supply. Another passive option is castor oil in the navel at night, his "umbilical castor capacitor." He describes the navel as an embryonic scar where nerves and vessels permanently cross, rich in nerve endings, and says castor oil's chiral, polar main fat sends a small current up the vagus that restarts the bicarbonate flush.

#### In practice

He starts with daily slow belly breathing, humming or chanting, and nasal breathing (his vagus-nerve-stimulation protocol). He adds a castor-oil pack on the navel at night. His TENS vagus protocol keeps settings gentle and stimulation strictly on the left side. The basalt, copper and other passive device therapies serve anyone who wants to avoid electrical current.
