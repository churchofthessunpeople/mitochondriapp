## Magnetism

### Field direction, local geology and what global maps miss
- **id:** earth-field-vs-geology
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** Kruse says cells respond to the direction of the local magnetic field and how fast it is changing, not its average strength, and that global field models and ground readings smooth those local changes away.
- **protocols:** magnetic-sleep-pad

Kruse argues that what your cells respond to is not how strong Earth's magnetic field is where you live, but which way it points and how quickly that direction is changing. Official maps average those local shifts away, so in his model a place can look normal on paper while its field is tilting in ways that undermine mitochondria, the energy-producing parts of every cell.

#### Direction matters more than strength

At any spot the field has a horizontal part that runs parallel to the ground (he writes it "Bh") and a vertical part that dives into it ("Bz"). In Kruse's model the horizontal part works like a shock absorber for the cell's "spin filter": his name for the way one-handed (chiral) proteins let through only electrons spinning in one direction, an effect physicists call CISS. When a local field tips toward vertical, he says, that filter fails even if total strength is unchanged, and a vertical-dominated zone has only a "razor-thin" margin when the compass direction shifts.

Declination, the angle between compass north and true north, is in his view a measure of mismatch. The body runs on two references at once: true north, fixed by the planet's spin, keeps time for body clocks and the rotation of body fluids, while magnetic north lines up electron spins. He calls the spinning planet the "stator," after the fixed part of an electric motor, and treats declination as the "error rate" between it and the wandering dynamo inside. As the gap widens, the two gears stop meshing, and the body's internal whirlpools (blood flow through the heart, the fluid around the brain and the mitochondrial motor) lose their timing, so heavy hydrogen is no longer cleared.

He applies this to real places. Citing the World Magnetic Model (the official field map used for navigation) and ESA's Swarm satellites, he says the US East Coast is losing its horizontal component quickly. The geometry he prefers is the one found nearer the magnetic equator: flat field lines, a strong total field and a compass that points close to true north.

The horizontal part also explains, for Kruse, why the Southern Hemisphere has always been the harder half. Field lines gather toward the north, which he calls the "input," and spread apart over the south, the "exhaust." Southern lands stayed livable only where a strong horizontal component acted as a "magnetic dam" holding protons in place, as in the belt from Perth to Sydney and across northern Australia, and he says that component is now collapsing.

#### Speed of change

Kruse puts even more weight on how fast the field changes. Geophysicists call the speeding-up of that change "secular acceleration," and sudden lurches "geomagnetic jerks." In the past, he argues, shifts were slow enough for each generation's body clock to recalibrate; today's pace is new. A chart showing a steady 50,000 nT (nanotesla, the unit of field strength) over a decade can therefore hide a jerk that tilts a horizontal field toward vertical within a year. A fixed offset between compass and true north may be survivable, he says; erratic wandering is the warning sign, because it means the outer core itself is unstable.

#### What global models miss

The standard models, the World Magnetic Model and the IGRF, are smooth mathematical averages built for navigation, and Kruse says they deliberately iron out regional accelerations. Mitochondria, in his view, register the local ripple as it happens, so the models produce a "linearization mirage" of stability. He sees the real driver as the "non-dipole" field: patches at the surface of Earth's core with reversed polarity, which he says are deep and long-lived (citing de Oliveira, 2024) and speed up local decay. Declination stays useful as a shortcut; he calls it a "geographic multiplier" rather than the root cause.

Timing makes this worse. The World Magnetic Model is refreshed only every five years, while non-dipole features such as the South Atlantic Anomaly move and pulse faster, so jerks like those of 2025–2026 appear only after the models are corrected. Ground readings have their own blind spot: local iron deposits, basalt and wireless fields form what he calls a "magnetic fog" over the core's signal, and the three-axis sensors in phones struggle to separate the horizontal from the vertical part. Only satellites above the crust, such as Swarm, can tell a global trend from local interference. Even they record strength rather than the field's twist and coherence, which he likens to measuring a tornado with a thermometer.

#### The brain senses direction

For proof that people register field direction, Kruse cites the 2019 Caltech study by Joseph Kirschvink and Connie Wang: alpha brain waves dropped by about 25% when the direction of an Earth-strength field was rotated, with its strength unchanged. He takes this to mean that the thalamus, the brain's relay center, works as a magnetic sensor. Because subjects responded to rotation in one direction only, he reads the human magnetic sense as magnetite-based and one-handed, with a blind spot that pole drift is now pushing many populations into.

Heavy hydrogen, he argues, dulls that sense further. He cites an *eLife* paper showing that a radical pair (two molecules sharing linked unpaired electrons, the basis of many proposed biological compasses) grows less sensitive to weak fields as "hyperfine" interactions with nearby atomic nuclei multiply. Deuterium's nucleus adds exactly that kind of noise, so in his model it blinds the sensor; he calls this the "hyperfine noise trap" and links it to birds dying as southern declination rises.

#### The rock under your feet

Local geology, Kruse says, modifies all of this. Volcanic or iron-rich rock carries magnetism frozen in when it formed and can hold a flat "micro-field" at the surface even while the regional field tilts; he names El Salvador's volcanic front and the iron-rich bedrock of New York's Adirondacks. Telluric currents, the natural electric currents in Earth's crust, travel along faults and fractures, and saltwater-soaked limestone can carry electrical noise for miles. For these reasons he judges places lot by lot rather than from a national map.

He sums this up as the "local stator" rule: global averages can no longer be trusted anywhere, and the rock underfoot decides. Because cooling lava records the field of its day, old rock can work against the present field. On Hawaii's Kīlauea, he says, compasses spin or point south because massive older basalt below set during the reversed field before the last full pole swap, the Brunhes–Matuyama reversal about 780,000 years ago, and it overpowers today's weaker field. Hawaii and New Zealand's North Island thus sit on a patchwork that he likens to standing on a reversed battery, with null zones and wildly varying strength: they have the mineral fuel (copper and iron for melanin enzymes) but a noisy ignition. He prefers thick crust of consistent normal polarity near zero declination, noting that Central America was laid down about 80 million years ago.

In practice, he tells people to look beneath the smoothed maps: compare Swarm data and secular-acceleration charts for their own zip code over time, and move to stable ground if the area shows a jerk or turns toward vertical. Where the local field is poor, the linked magnetic sleep pad protocol uses a static magnet pad at night as a personal stand-in, though he warns that a strong vertical field at night and a very different field by day can confuse the brain's sense of direction.

### Grounding, and why the ground matters
- **id:** barefoot-earth
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** Kruse says barefoot contact with conductive earth feeds the body electrons that keep oxygen in its safe state, but how much it helps depends on the ground, your posture and the local magnetic field.
- **protocols:** barefoot-earth

Grounding, or "earthing," means skin-to-earth contact: bare feet on soil, sand or rock, or in the sea. Kruse argues it gives mitochondria a steady electrical reference that indoor life removes, and that this matters more as Earth's magnetic field weakens. In his model, though, grounding is only as good as the ground you stand on, and it cannot replace a good magnetic field.

#### What grounding does

Kruse describes the planet as a battery: the churning iron core (the geodynamo) keeps a large reservoir of free electrons at the surface, and the body is conductive enough to draw on it. Those electrons, he says, keep oxygen in its "triplet" state, its normal and stable spin arrangement, instead of letting it flip into reactive "singlet" oxygen. He adds that grounding restores Zeeman splitting (the way a magnetic field separates electron energy levels) in heme, the iron-containing core of blood and respiratory proteins, which he calls the body's magnetic antenna.

The result, in his account, is a steady electrical baseline for the inner mitochondrial membrane, the surface where energy is made, that bypasses swings in the local field. That is why he treats grounding as a stabilizer where the field is declining or shifting, and why he says sleeping grounded lets the body's night-time repair circuit run.

He links this to Robert Becker's "current of injury," the small natural current that steers tissue repair, and to a surgeon's chance finding that a weak current can reshape cartilage. The Hunza, he says, get such currents free from their mountains' steady natural field and the sun, which keeps their collagen and elastin supple. He pictures the grounded body as a pyramid, the brain at its apex catching light and the feet as its base on the earth; cut off the ground and, in his phrase, you become a "Maillard oven," slowly browning from inside the way food browns when cooked.

#### Only half the solution

Grounding fixes only half the problem, Kruse argues. Melatonin, which cells make at night as an antioxidant shield, needs both electric and magnetic containment. Grounding restores the electric side, but magnetic disorder from a weak field or wireless devices is harder to block. So in a magnetic "dead zone," or where declination changes steeply, standing on grass is not enough on its own.

#### Not all ground is equal

The type of ground matters. Kruse's concern here is not Wi-Fi, which water absorbs quickly, but low-frequency electrical noise such as power-grid leakage and stray currents. Limestone riddled with water channels and soaked with saltwater (karst) conducts that hum sideways for miles, so grounding on it near resorts or heavy infrastructure is, he says, like grounding to a live wire. He calls it a "voltage siphon" that drains the body's charge. Quiet rural karst is less of a problem, but he still rates Florida and Mexico's Yucatán as poor places to ground during the decline. Dense basalt, by contrast, insulates against that sideways noise and connects you to the steady local charge of the rock.

#### Posture at dawn

How you ground matters too. At the beach he prefers only the feet and ankles in wet sand or shallow surf, with as much skin as possible in open air. Water absorbs the near-infrared light of sunrise and, he says, acts like a shield, so neck-deep immersion blocks the dawn signal the skin is trying to catch.

His fullest version he calls "mammalian sphinxing": facing east at sunrise on all fours, so that hands as well as feet, with their sweat glands, touch the earth. The extra electrons, he says, let the body capture more of the dawn light, provided its water is in the structured state he scores at about 160 on the dielectric scale (a measure of how well water stores charge) rather than the 78 of ordinary water; sunlight itself pushes that number up. In his account this lowers the body's need to pull electrons from food and quiets mTOR, the cell's main growth signal, at no cost.

Even occasional contact counts in his model. People who sometimes ground or catch morning sun show a different disease pattern from those who never do, which he calls the "Earth–Sun circuit breaker": the earth supplies electrons that restore the charge of blood-vessel linings, while sunrise infrared frees cytochrome c oxidase, the mitochondrial enzyme that hands electrons to oxygen. He says people with collagen disorders typically get too little of both.

In practice, the barefoot grounding protocol asks for 20–60 minutes a day barefoot in surf, wet sand or dewy grass or on basalt, ideally at dawn, with basalt or wet volcanic black sand preferred. Avoid salt-soaked limestone near resorts or big power lines, check a spot with a meter if stray current worries you, never join dissimilar metals in a grounding connection, and use a low-resistance indoor grounding system as the fallback.

### Volcanic ground, basalt and passive anchors
- **id:** volcanic-basalt-pinning
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** Kruse says magnetic volcanic rock locks in a flat local field and holds water and clouds in place, so grounding on basalt beats any device, and passive stone or copper anchors beat powered ones.
- **protocols:** barefoot-earth, el-salvador-guide, copper-ring-basalt, volcano-protocol

"Volcano Rx" is Kruse's name for living on, and grounding barefoot on, magnetic volcanic rock. In his model basalt holds a stable, flat magnetic field at the surface even when the planet's field weakens or tilts overhead, which makes volcanic regions refuges during the current decline. He ranks this above any device and offers passive stone and copper tools for people who cannot yet live there.

#### How basalt holds a flat field

Kruse starts from the rock itself. Lava from the mantle is rich in iron, and as it cools below about 550 °C, its Curie temperature (the point below which a mineral can hold magnetism), tiny crystals of magnetite and titanomagnetite form and line up like compass needles with the field of that moment. Frozen into basalt, they keep that direction permanently. More than 90% of volcanic rock is basalt, so large volcanoes and lava tubes create strong local magnetic anomalies.

Drawing on Yannick van Doorne's work on basalt, he adds that the stone's iron and magnesium make it paramagnetic, so it gathers field lines and concentrates them. The effect depends on the stone's tie to the regional field, though: spreading basalt sand through a house with no connection to the larger field, he says, "makes zero sense."

Where the regional field is tipping toward vertical, Kruse says, this frozen magnetism bends the field lines flat and creates a horizontal micro-field at ground level, a "lithospheric straightener" that holds even if the agonic line moves or the core weakens. Grounding barefoot on basalt then supplies, in his account, a dense stream of earth-current electrons that charge the mitochondrial membrane, help ATP synthase (the rotary enzyme that makes the cell's energy currency) repel deuterium, or heavy hydrogen, and support melanin. Beside this he calls PEMF (pulsed electromagnetic field) devices the "loser option."

Not every volcanic place works. Hawaii's rock, he argues, froze during the reversed field that came before the Brunhes–Matuyama pole swap about 780,000 years ago, so it can cancel today's field instead of adding to it; El Salvador's younger volcanic rock carries no memory of that flip. Active volcanoes pin best, because every magnetic field needs moving charge, and he likens them to superconductors that lock field lines in place. His own homes sit over water-filled lava tubes that he likens to a dynamo.

#### Cloud caps and black sand

Kruse reads the sky over a volcano as a gauge. The rock, he says, gathers the field around the cone, and water molecules, being tiny magnets themselves, are drawn in. A cloud that sits on the summit for long stretches, such as the "capilla" clouds over Nicaragua's Ometepe or El Salvador's volcanoes, is in his words water "pinned to the stator," and it shields the people below from cosmic rays. He says the Maya chose sites near volcanoes and cenotes (natural sinkhole wells) where clouds linger, because that water is lighter in deuterium, the idea behind the "living water" of Viktor Schauberger and Rudolf Steiner.

The coast adds a second battery. El Salvador's black-sand beaches are grains of magnetite and ilmenite that keep the field strength of the eruptions that made them. When salty waves, which conduct electricity, wash over that sand, their motion induces a small field of its own, so in his view the shoreline is a high-flux zone that resists the South Atlantic Anomaly and resets the protons of anyone standing on it.

#### Why El Salvador

Kruse calls El Salvador a "geological ark." It lies near the magnetic equator (about 13°N), where the field already runs flat; its fault zone and volcanic chain run east to west, matching those flat lines; and deep below the surface lavas sit slowly cooled magma bodies full of very stable magnetite that act as a hidden anchor. Together, he says, these work as a "spatial shear deflector" that keeps a weakening field from tilting vertical. He adds that the country has sat near an agonic line (zero declination) for about 11,500 years, since the end of the Younger Dryas cold period, and that all of it qualifies because it is volcanic throughout. Elsewhere, iron-rich bedrock such as New York's Adirondacks offers a partial refuge.

He also points to heat from below. More than 20 volcanoes have been active within the last 10,000 years, and their young lava and hot-water vents create local highs and lows in the field that, he says, filter out noise and steady the local signal; geothermal plants such as Berlín and Ahuachapán supply over a fifth of the country's energy. He drills his own wells into deuterium-depleted water from the volcano. Even the earthquakes help in his account: the regular shaking works as a "mechanical dither," a jiggle that clears background noise so the body's clock genes can pick up Earth's rhythm.

#### Ancient builders

Kruse believes earlier cultures understood this. He reads the six tons of perforated magnetite and ilmenite blocks found at the Olmec site of San Lorenzo as a deliberate grid to restore a stable reference field when the dynamo sagged. Megaliths, dolmens and long barrows of granite, basalt and quartz-rich stone were, in his view, "paramagnetic sponges" (paramagnetic meaning weakly drawn into a magnetic field) that concentrate a weakening field inside the chamber like a lens. Built on earth-current lines and faults, their quartz was squeezed by tidal stress into making small currents, and people sheltered or were buried in such stone during past excursions.

Pyramids, in his reading, were "reverse volcanoes." The Maya, he says, began in volcanic El Salvador and, as they moved north away from the volcanoes, built pyramids to copy their effect, with observatories beside them. He cites the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus (about 1650 BCE), the Great Pyramid's precise true-north alignment, a 2018 *Journal of Applied Physics* model in which a pyramid concentrates radio energy in its chambers and beneath its base, and Alexander Golod's large fiberglass pyramids. Built over cenotes and aquifers, such structures in his model spun deuterium out of the water table, leaving lighter water for drinking and crops. As the field kept falling after Egypt's Old Kingdom, Sudan's Kushites built 255 smaller, steeper pyramids (about 70°) to strengthen the effect; when the magnetic anomaly over the Yucatán grew too large for the Maya pyramids to correct, that civilization dispersed. He floats El Salvador's planned Bitcoin City, at the foot of the Conchagua volcano, as a possible modern "magnetic pyramid."

#### Passive anchors: stone and copper

For people not yet on volcanic ground, Kruse prefers passive tools to powered stimulators that push "harsh, non-native" current into tissue. While grounded, rest a smooth basalt stone or volcanic clay behind the ear or on the temple, which he calls a "zero-frequency amplifier," or keep a paramagnetic stone in the pillow. Another option is an open copper ring about 30 cm (12 inches) across worn around the head or neck. He traces it to Georges Lakhovsky, who in 1925 at the Salpêtrière Hospital in Paris placed a single open copper ring around a geranium infected with tumor-causing bacteria; in Kruse's account that plant recovered while the untreated plants died. He also credits Slim Spurling's copper coils.

In his model such rings are passive antennas for the Schumann resonance (the natural 7.83 Hz electromagnetic hum between the ground and the upper atmosphere) and for geomagnetic waves. They raise cell voltage, help cells shed heavy isotopes, and let copper stand in for melanin where it is thin. The neck is his preferred target, his "vagal vortex," where the carotid arteries, the vagus nerve and structured water run together.

He explains the open ring as an inductance–capacitance circuit, the simplest kind of tuned antenna, which gathers frequencies from the dynamo and the sun and concentrates them in one spot. Lakhovsky described sick tissue oscillating at lower frequencies, falling from 72 to 42 MHz; Kruse attributes that drop to deuterium, which in his view weighs down the natural vibrations of DNA, proteins and collagen. Lakhovsky's later Multi-Wave Oscillator, with its broad spread of frequencies, let every part find its resonance and, in Kruse's reading, shake the deuterium loose.

In practice, the barefoot grounding protocol says to ground on basalt or wet volcanic black sand. The El Salvador guide lists his preferred spots near active volcanoes such as Ilopango and says to test any lot with mosquitoes and candles before buying. The basalt-and-copper-ring protocol covers the stone, ring and pillow options, which he frames as a bridge while you fix your location. His Volcano Protocol copies a volcano's answer at body scale, pairing life in a pinned, low-latitude place and sunrise grounding on volcanic ground with steps to shake deuterium from the spine and build up carbon dioxide.

### Choosing a place to live
- **id:** choosing-a-location
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** Kruse calls location the final arbiter of mitochondrial health and gives three field numbers, a way to read the declination map, geology to avoid and on-the-ground tests for choosing it.
- **protocols:** relocation-checklist, el-salvador-guide

For Kruse, where you live finally decides how well your mitochondria can run: no supplement, diet or exercise plan can outrun land that has lost its magnetic footing. He gives three numbers to look up, a way to read the declination map and tests for a specific lot, and he says your location sets how hard you must work every day to stay well.

#### The three numbers

His checklist uses the World Magnetic Model, the official field map. Total intensity, the field's strength in nanotesla (nT), should be as high as possible: above about 45,000 nT, ideally up to 60,000. Inclination, the angle at which field lines dip into the ground, should be 0–45°; flat lines, he says, anchor the body's internal axes, while lines plunging steeply toward the poles destabilize them. Declination, the angle between compass north and true north, should be close to 0°. A line of zero declination is called an agonic line, and on it there is no twist between the planet's spin axis and its field. He also says to avoid the South Atlantic Anomaly, a huge weak patch where intensity falls below about 22,000 nT and which, he says, now reaches the Caribbean.

The best places, in his model, are where an agonic line crosses a volcanic chain at a latitude with strong sun: his "fountain of youth coordinate." He notes that the North American agonic line runs down through the Midwest toward the Gulf and lines up with the Pacific's volcanoes, but the Midwest itself lacks volcanic ground. Agonic lines move with the poles, so he says to follow them rather than latitude, and that the best places will never be fixed; there are "no islands or oases."

Even so, he warns against chasing an agonic line by itself. The goal is a place where the body can keep making melanin and sorting heavy from light hydrogen, which needs all three legs of his stool: light, water and magnetism. Picking a spot only because the agonic line crosses stable ground leads into what he calls a "Mayan trap."

#### Reading the maps

In his reading of the declination map, green (the agonic line) is best, red (east declination) acceptable and bold dark blue (strong west declination) very bad. Where the lines bunch together the field changes over short distances, which in his view shifts the alignment of mitochondrial membranes as you move around; that alone is a reason to leave. He compares his town's ESA Swarm satellite map on the same date across years to see which way it is trending.

The standard UV index, he says, is useless for this, because it ignores declination and the local deuterium load. Sydney and Perth can share a UV reading, yet in his view Sydney, under steadier field conditions, works like a "quantum processor," while Perth, on the approach of the South Atlantic Anomaly, is a "Maillard oven" that browns tissue. What people need, he argues, is a "Dynamo-Isotopic Calculator" that weighs sun, field and deuterium together.

Deserts give strong light but a weak magnetic background, and dry sand gives no ground, so he advises against raising children there. Some still-green land, he adds, already carries a desert-grade weak field; he names North America's breadbasket.

#### Faults and plate boundaries

The geology beneath a region can rule it out. Kruse singles out the New Madrid Seismic Zone between Memphis and St. Louis, which sits on a failed rift (the Reelfoot Rift) over hot, wet, weak mantle. Unlike the frozen magnetism of old lava, he calls it an active "induction zone": citing radio direction-finding research, he says the fault gives off broad-band electromagnetic signals under crustal stress, so the local field turns spiky and the body's repair window unreliable. He spent 18 months in nearby Cape Girardeau watching the zone affect people, which confirmed his 2013 move out of middle Tennessee, made before he had good magnetic maps. He is similarly wary of the Azores, where the North American, Eurasian and African plates meet, a junction he says is being loaded by the East African Rift shoving the African plate west.

#### Testing a lot

Before buying land, Kruse reads what lives there. Mosquitoes active by day as well as night, a 24-hour beeswax candle that burns out early and a faltering local water cycle all warn of a weak spot; he used mosquitoes and candles to choose his own property. A "smart" house full of wireless devices, he says, cancels even a good location.

#### Where he says no, and where he says yes

He rules out California, which he says has a "double deficit" of high declination (about +11–12°) and too little steady UV-B sunlight, and calls the West Coast and Texas "cooked," with the East Coast next. He adds Florida, the Louisiana coast, Tennessee, Kansas, Colorado (a mile up, closer to the radiation belts), Hawaii, Minnesota (a decent but falling field and only about three months of useful sun), the UK, Germany, Mexico's Yucatán and Australia. Florida's Atlantic coast is fed by the weakening Atlantic overturning current (AMOC), which for him is a deal-breaker, and as that current fails he expects people east of the Mississippi to load up on deuterium even when doing much right. Further afield he names South America, which he calls the most dangerous place to be because the anomaly is swallowing it, continental Europe, Japan (which shares Hawaii's Pacific-basin rock signal) and New Zealand's South Island, which he says worsened within a year of his visit. In the US he names only the Pacific Northwest. He rates the Hong Kong area, volcanically active at the start of the Ring of Fire, as good apart from its crowding. His own choice is El Salvador's volcanic coast, though not every spot there is equal; he avoids crowded, wireless-heavy beaches such as El Zonte.

#### After the move

Kruse warns that people can feel worse after moving somewhere better. Borrowing Robert Becker's idea of "adaptive healing," he says tissue has to rebuild for the new environment, much as running modern current through 1920s wiring can burn a house down. People of northern descent, he adds, should adapt to tropical sun gradually.

In practice, the relocation checklist gathers his criteria: the three World Magnetic Model numbers, avoiding the South Atlantic Anomaly, reading declination and Swarm maps, steering clear of stressed faults such as New Madrid, grounding on basalt or wet black sand, testing lots with mosquitoes, candles and the water cycle, no smart house, and ground-floor living. The El Salvador guide adds his local notes: buy an empty lot and build, favor areas near active volcanoes, and use the Costa del Sol beach for sunrise grounding.

### Haplogroup and the "magnetic zipcode"
- **id:** haplogroup-zipcode
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** Kruse says your maternal mitochondrial lineage sets the gearing of your energy engine and so decides which local field and sun suit you, and a mismatch speeds aging.
- **protocols:** four-phase-sun-day, relocation-checklist

A haplogroup is a maternal lineage traced through mitochondrial DNA, which passes from mother to child. Kruse calls it the blueprint and your "magnetic zipcode," the field where you live, the dial. When the two don't match, he argues, aging speeds up, so the same place can suit one person and harm another.

He frames it as a chain: the haplogroup is the blueprint, Marcus theory (the physics of how fast electrons jump between molecules) is the law, the magnetic zipcode is the dial, and disease is the failure that follows a mismatch. Lineages arose, he says, as answers to past changes in Earth's field. ### The planet's story: excursions, the magnetic forge and volcanic CO₂
- **id:** geomagnetic-excursions-evolution
- **pillar:** magnetism

Kruse reads Earth's magnetic history as the large-scale version of the physics he sees inside cells. In his model, periods when the field steadied allowed complex life, periods when it faltered reshaped or culled it, and the weakening under way now is another such turning point: a planetary re-tuning rather than an extinction.

#### Excursions and reversals

A reversal is a full swap of the magnetic poles; it takes about 2,000–10,000 years and then holds for 100,000 years or more. An excursion is an aborted reversal: the field weakens and wanders for a few centuries up to about 2,000 years, then recovers. The Laschamp excursion about 41,000 years ago and the Mono Lake excursion are examples. Seafloor magnetic stripes smooth excursions out, Kruse notes, but fast-settling lake and deep-sea sediments record hundreds of them, along with spikes in beryllium-10 and carbon-14 that mark extra cosmic rays. Reversals, in his view, cause extinctions because ozone is stripped for thousands of years; excursions select survivors. The field has collapsed about 500 times with only five mass extinctions, he says, but he believes several human civilizations were lost to such events.

Direct measurement of field strength began with Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1832. Earlier values come from fired pottery and lava, whose iron minerals lock in the field of the moment they cool, and researchers stitch those records into regional and global models. They show, Kruse points out, that swings can be fast: in the Levantine Iron Age Anomaly (Shaar and colleagues, 2016), the field over the Near East spiked to about double what models predict around 980 BCE and again in the early 8th century BCE, then quickly halved.

He reads the Permian "Great Dying," which killed about 96% of life, as a magnetic event in which heavy isotopes piled up while the field faltered. The survivors, in his account, were "hard" life that could keep its internal spin order with its own melanin and DHA, an "internal sun."

#### A steady field made complex life

Kruse argues that complex life arrived only once Earth's dynamo steadied. During the "Boring Billion," the long, quiet middle of Earth's history, a weak and unstable field let the solar wind strip ozone, leaving the surface scorched by UV; he cites modelling by Cooke and colleagues that puts ozone below 120 Dobson units when oxygen was 1% of today's level. Oxygen, he says, kept flipping into its reactive singlet form. Just before the Cambrian came what he calls a "magnetic flatline": in the Ediacaran period the field was 10–30 times weaker than today, hydrogen bled into space, water grew heavier and life stayed simple.

The Cambrian explosion, when about 32 animal body plans appeared, began in his account when the inner core hardened, the field stabilized and oxygen locked into its stable triplet state, letting one-handed molecules such as collagen and melanin act as spin filters. He cites a June 2025 *Science Advances* study linking atmospheric oxygen to the strength of Earth's main field over 540 million years, with joint peaks between 330 and 220 million years ago, the window in which he says the mammalian blueprint was built. He argues that Nick Lane's oxygen-centered explanation misses this, citing 2025–2026 genomic studies in *Nature* (publicized by NIOZ and the University of Copenhagen) showing that complex-cell machinery arose before oxygen was abundant. He also notes Sweetman and colleagues' finding (*Nature Geoscience*) that metal-rich nodules on the deep seabed make "dark oxygen" without sunlight, and asks why, if that ran all along, life locked onto the field only at the Cambrian; his answer turns on the formation of the ozone layer.

#### Laschamp and modern humans

At Laschamp, Kruse says, the field fell below 5% of normal and auroras reached the equator. He links the event to the "chromosome 2 fuse," the spot where two ancestral ape chromosomes fused in humans, which sits beside genes for melanin, fetal hemoglobin and metabolism; in his model it tripped and reset human metabolism. Neanderthals, he says, "short-circuited" rather than lost a war, while modern humans emerged with a larger melanin shield and a burst of cognition and cave art. He also ties excursions to collagen failure, pointing to elongated skulls such as Peru's Paracas infants, and calls today's rise in joint hypermobility a planetary effect.

#### The magnetic forge today

Kruse frames current changes as planetary rebalancing, not something humans caused. Earth, he says, is leaving a sunlight-dominated era for a "magnetic forge" like Laschamp's. He puts the present at about 9% of a Laschamp-scale collapse; at 30–50% he expects Earth's core and outer shell to decouple, his "Tau" stage. He cites Madsen and colleagues (University of Edinburgh, 2026), who used Swarm and other satellite data to show that molten iron under the equatorial Pacific switched from westward to eastward flow in 2010 and has weakened since about 2020. He follows the ECDO theory, which proposes a sudden slip of Earth's outer shell, as a staged cycle he believes has begun.

A failing dynamo, he says, sheds its energy as heat and radio waves. Electrical resistance in the core's currents (Ohmic heating) releases about 1–2 terawatts, which strengthens mantle plumes and makes quakes likelier in already risky areas, while radio bursts picked up by the ANITA balloon experiment over Antarctica are in his reading the dynamo dumping its disorder into space at the pole. He also points to reports that the Chandler wobble, a small natural wobble of Earth's spin axis, has dropped by about 98%, which he reads as the planet's transmission slipping into neutral.

#### Oceans, weather and volcanoes

Moving, salty ocean currents, the Atlantic and Southern overturning circulations (AMOC and SMOC), make their own magnetic fields and, in his model, are anchored to the dynamo. The link is the Lorentz force, the push a magnetic field gives to moving charged particles such as those in seawater; a weaker field gives the current less torque, so it stalls, loses oxygen and stagnates, which is why he calls the AMOC the planet's "vagal exhaust." As the currents slow, he reads stalled currents, a silent Atlantic hurricane season, a hyperactive Pacific and harder El Niño "sloshing" as magnetic effects; a collapsing AMOC, he says, keeps loading people in Florida with deuterium. Mining the deep-sea nodules for EV batteries, he warns, removes natural "geobatteries" that keep deep water in good order.

The Arctic tells the same story. He reads mosquitoes newly found in Iceland, and an Arctic warming about four times faster than the rest of the world, as a "magnetic Arctic flashover": as the north pole races toward Siberia, the Arctic loses the field's hold on its air and water, so "climate change" is in his phrase a software name for a hardware failure. Fish die-offs in Norway at 50–60°N, arriving far earlier than he expected, are further canaries; a fish's single tube-shaped heart works in his view as a vortex tied to the field that stalls as it weakens. He welcomes this countermeasure and says humans need more CO₂, not less, during an excursion; he reads the rise from about 370 to 426 ppm, and a 5% greening of the planet roughly equal to one more Amazon, as a living shield against field loss. Finally, he maps planets onto cell states in his "Universal Scaling Law" (gas giants healthy, Earth stressed, Venus cancerous, Mars necrotic). Mars lost its dynamo, and in his account its water lost its magnetic hold, grew heavy with deuterium and boiled away into space, so colonizing it, he says, would be lethal.

He rejects "balance" or moderation during an excursion, and the planet-scale story is why he puts location ahead of every other health choice.

### Magnets, pads and powered devices
- **id:** magnetico-sleep-pad

Kruse sees artificial magnetic fields as a two-edged tool. A static magnetic sleep pad can stand in for a weakening planetary field, but the wrong magnet, pole or strength can "over-clamp" tissue, and every device ranks behind location, sunlight and grounding.

#### Sleep pads

Where the field is collapsing outright, he sees little point either. Magnet makers, Kruse says, ignore polarity. The north pole in his model calms and organizes electron spins, while the south pole speeds activity and can tip oxygen into its reactive singlet form. #### Dose has a window

Kruse cites a 2025 study by Beutner and colleagues in which isolated rat-heart mitochondria exposed to a static field far weaker than a fridge magnet raised their respiration by about 40% while producing fewer free radicals, but only within a narrow range; stronger fields lost the effect. ### Reading the field at home and in the body
- **id:** field-proxies
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** Kruse's do-it-yourself readouts of a place's magnetic quality and the body's state: beeswax candles, mosquitoes, clouds, animals, tans, blood markers, heart-rate variability and a personal scorecard.
- **protocols:** beeswax-candle-test, hrv-tracking, track-your-field

Kruse treats human health as the most sensitive magnetometer there is: fertility, cancer and deaths tracked by zip code, he says, show field change before official data do. Because those data are smoothed and slow, he gives members simple ways to read a place and their own body.

#### Reading a place

His best-known proxy is a rated beeswax candle burned where you live. On schedule means normal; faster, such as a 24-hour candle gone in 18, means field decline or a heavy wireless load; slower and clean means a strong local field; slower with leftover wax means an artificial "over-clamp," as when a member set a candle on a magnetic bracelet. He uses only beeswax, which he describes as deuterium-depleted and made of one-handed molecules, because paraffin is heavy in deuterium and spoils the reading. When the disturbance is visibly overhead, such as auroras above Finland, the test is moot.

On land he pairs candles with mosquitoes: insects active by day as well as night mark a weak spot, a method he calls "magnetic dowsing" and used to buy property in El Salvador. Insects work as sensors, he explains, because their chitin shells are piezoelectric (they turn pressure and fields into tiny currents), so mosquitoes spreading north, as they now have into Iceland, show that the magnetic barrier that kept them out has given way. In a humid region the sky offers another clue: a volcano that has lost its cloud cap signals a local "magnetic stall," a cone no longer able to hold its water.

Animals tell the same story. In his account, traditional trackers read land by behavior, not head counts: large herds splintering into small erratic groups, dull dry coats even near water, and grazing shifted to pre-dawn and dusk. His shortlist of signs that a place is failing is aurorae, sick people and dying animals, especially birds, bees and fish; he watches reports from his Gulf of Mexico shrimp supplier and names godwits, Arctic terns and albatrosses as bird canaries. Trees and animals, he adds, feel the ground currents and gases a stressed fault releases days to weeks before a quake. He also suggests long pipelines as a citizen's magnetometer, because their electrical potential swings when earth currents surge.

Instruments have limits. Even a high-grade ground magnetometer, he says, cannot see what he is tracking, which has to be measured from above by the Swarm satellites; for better ground readings he names the optically pumped magnetometer. Unable to get Swarm numbers for two years, he took three world cruises in two years to see the Southern Ocean and its people for himself.

#### Reading the body

In the blood, a rapidly shifting potassium level means, to Kruse, that the nearest agonic line (where compass north and true north agree) is moving away or that the person is spoiling their own magnetic tracking. High ferritin, the iron-storage marker, reflects a weak local field and poor choices around light and grounding. His four everyday proxies are morning blood sugar (his "magnetic dawning phenomenon"), LDL, vitamin B12 and new pigment changes. A mitochondrial enzyme (OXPHOS) test can be read as a map of your location's field, and a muscle biopsy viewed under an electron microscope shows what a place does to the shape of your mitochondria.

A tan shows only the outside, though it can still test official numbers: his clients in Calgary all report they cannot hold a tan, which makes him doubt the 56,000 nT figure quoted for the area. For what he calls the "interior tan," the state of melanin and water deep inside, he looks at homocysteine and TMAO (which he treats as "silt" markers of poor deuterium clearance), white-matter changes on MRI and slow flow on thermal imaging, which he calls the ideal fast test because it reflects how thick the blood is. He predicts that a zip-code map of homocysteine would overlay the declination map, peaking in weak-field zones such as the US West and Midwest and the South Atlantic Anomaly. He considers epigenetic clocks and saliva telomere tests unreliable during the decline.

#### His problem-solving method

Kruse adapts George Pólya's 1945 book *How to Solve It*, as his neurosurgery mentors taught it, into four steps. First, define the problem as a local voltage drop and check for AC magnetic fields and blue light. Second, work back from oxygen in its stable triplet state, restoring the field with grounding, sun and natural resonators such as basalt. Third, act with targeted cold, breathing and light, patiently. Fourth, look back: check the result by another route, write down the lessons for the next case, and judge progress on a personal scorecard: salivary D/H (the ratio of deuterium to ordinary hydrogen in saliva) below 130 ppm, ultra-weak light given off by the body turning quiet and coherent, and nerve voltage back near −90 mV, with heart-rate variability (HRV, the beat-to-beat variation in heart timing) in deep sleep as the running gauge. HRV, he cautions, cannot warn of a sudden local loss of field, the kind he blames for people dropping dead while exercising.

In practice, the beeswax candle test protocol uses a rated pure-beeswax candle and reads it alongside day-versus-night mosquito activity. The HRV tracking protocol makes deep-sleep HRV the main progress gauge, adds the everyday markers and scorecard above, and treats high ferritin and fast-shifting potassium as warnings.
