## Magnetism

### Field direction, local geology and what global maps miss
- **id:** earth-field-vs-geology
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** Kruse says cells respond to the direction of the local magnetic field and how fast it is changing, not its average strength, and that global field models smooth those local changes away.
- **protocols:** magnetic-sleep-pad

Kruse argues that what your cells respond to is not how strong Earth's magnetic field is where you live, but which way it points and how quickly that direction is changing. Official maps average those local shifts away, so in his model a place can look normal on paper while its field is tilting in ways that undermine mitochondria, the energy-producing parts of every cell.

#### Direction matters more than strength

At any spot the field has a horizontal part that runs parallel to the ground (he writes it "Bh") and a vertical part that dives into it ("Bz"). In Kruse's model the horizontal part works like a shock absorber for the cell's "spin filter": his name for the way one-handed (chiral) proteins let through only electrons spinning in one direction, an effect physicists call CISS. When a local field tips toward vertical, he says, that filter fails even if total strength is unchanged, and a vertical-dominated zone has only a "razor-thin" margin when the compass direction shifts.

He applies this to real places. Citing the World Magnetic Model (the official field map used for navigation) and ESA's Swarm satellites, he says the US East Coast is losing its horizontal component quickly. The geometry he prefers is the one found nearer the magnetic equator: flat field lines, a strong total field and a compass that points close to true north.

#### Speed of change

Kruse puts even more weight on how fast the field changes. Geophysicists call the speeding-up of that change "secular acceleration," and sudden lurches "geomagnetic jerks." In the past, he argues, shifts were slow enough for each generation's body clock to recalibrate; today's pace is new. A chart showing a steady 50,000 nT (nanotesla, the unit of field strength) over a decade can therefore hide a jerk that tilts a horizontal field toward vertical within a year.

#### What global models miss

The standard models, the World Magnetic Model and the IGRF, are smooth mathematical averages built for navigation, and Kruse says they deliberately iron out regional accelerations. Mitochondria, in his view, register the local ripple as it happens, so the models produce a "linearization mirage" of stability. He sees the real driver as the "non-dipole" field: patches at the surface of Earth's core with reversed polarity, which he says are deep and long-lived (citing de Oliveira, 2024) and speed up local decay. Declination, the angle between compass north and true north, stays useful as a shortcut; he calls it a "geographic multiplier" rather than the root cause.

#### The brain senses direction

For proof that people register field direction, Kruse cites the 2019 Caltech study by Joseph Kirschvink and Connie Wang: alpha brain waves dropped by about 25% when the direction of an Earth-strength field was rotated, with its strength unchanged. He takes this to mean that the thalamus, the brain's relay center, works as a magnetic sensor. Because subjects responded to rotation in one direction only, he reads the human magnetic sense as magnetite-based and one-handed, with a blind spot that pole drift is now pushing many populations into.

#### The rock under your feet

Local geology, Kruse says, modifies all of this. Volcanic or iron-rich rock carries magnetism frozen in when it formed and can hold a flat "micro-field" at the surface even while the regional field tilts; he names El Salvador's volcanic front and the iron-rich bedrock of New York's Adirondacks. Telluric currents, the natural electric currents in Earth's crust, travel along faults and fractures, and saltwater-soaked limestone can carry electrical noise for miles. For these reasons he judges places lot by lot rather than from a national map.

In practice, he tells people to look beneath the smoothed maps: compare Swarm data and secular-acceleration charts for their own zip code over time, and move to stable ground if the area shows a jerk or turns toward vertical. Where the local field is poor, the linked magnetic sleep pad protocol uses a static magnet pad at night as a personal stand-in, though he warns that a strong vertical field at night and a very different field by day can confuse the brain's sense of direction.

### Grounding, and why the ground matters
- **id:** barefoot-earth
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** Kruse says barefoot contact with conductive earth feeds the body electrons that keep oxygen in its safe state, but how much it helps depends on the ground, your posture and the local magnetic field.
- **protocols:** barefoot-earth

Grounding, or "earthing," means skin-to-earth contact: bare feet on soil, sand or rock, or in the sea. Kruse argues it gives mitochondria a steady electrical reference that indoor life removes, and that this matters more as Earth's magnetic field weakens. In his model, though, grounding is only as good as the ground you stand on, and it cannot replace a good magnetic field.

#### What grounding does

Kruse describes the planet as a battery: the churning iron core (the geodynamo) keeps a large reservoir of free electrons at the surface, and the body is conductive enough to draw on it. Those electrons, he says, keep oxygen in its "triplet" state, its normal and stable spin arrangement, instead of letting it flip into reactive "singlet" oxygen. He adds that grounding restores Zeeman splitting (the way a magnetic field separates electron energy levels) in heme, the iron-containing core of blood and respiratory proteins, which he calls the body's magnetic antenna.

The result, in his account, is a steady electrical baseline for the inner mitochondrial membrane, the surface where energy is made, that bypasses swings in the local field. That is why he treats grounding as a stabilizer where the field is declining or shifting, and why he says sleeping grounded lets the body's night-time repair circuit run.

#### Only half the solution

Grounding fixes only half the problem, Kruse argues. Melatonin, which cells make at night as an antioxidant shield, needs both electric and magnetic containment. Grounding restores the electric side, but magnetic disorder from a weak field or wireless devices is harder to block. So in a magnetic "dead zone," or where declination changes steeply, standing on grass is not enough on its own.

#### Not all ground is equal

The type of ground matters. Kruse's concern here is not Wi-Fi, which water absorbs quickly, but low-frequency electrical noise such as power-grid leakage and stray currents. Limestone riddled with water channels and soaked with saltwater (karst) conducts that hum sideways for miles, so grounding on it near resorts or heavy infrastructure is, he says, like grounding to a live wire. He calls it a "voltage siphon" that drains the body's charge. Quiet rural karst is less of a problem, but he still rates Florida and Mexico's Yucatán as poor places to ground during the decline. Dense basalt, by contrast, insulates against that sideways noise and connects you to the steady local charge of the rock.

#### Posture at dawn

How you ground matters too. At the beach he prefers only the feet and ankles in wet sand or shallow surf, with as much skin as possible in open air. Water absorbs the near-infrared light of sunrise and, he says, acts like a shield, so neck-deep immersion blocks the dawn signal the skin is trying to catch.

Even occasional contact counts in his model. People who sometimes ground or catch morning sun show a different disease pattern from those who never do, which he calls the "Earth–Sun circuit breaker": the earth supplies electrons that restore the charge of blood-vessel linings, while sunrise infrared frees cytochrome c oxidase, the mitochondrial enzyme that hands electrons to oxygen. He says people with collagen disorders typically get too little of both.

In practice, the barefoot grounding protocol asks for 20–60 minutes a day barefoot in surf, wet sand or dewy grass or on basalt, ideally at dawn, with basalt or wet volcanic black sand preferred. Avoid salt-soaked limestone near resorts or big power lines, check a spot with a meter if stray current worries you, never join dissimilar metals in a grounding connection, and use a low-resistance indoor grounding system as the fallback.

### Volcanic ground, basalt and passive anchors
- **id:** volcanic-basalt-pinning
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** Kruse says magnetic volcanic rock locks in a flat local field, so grounding on basalt beats any device, and passive stone or copper anchors beat powered ones.
- **protocols:** barefoot-earth, el-salvador-guide, copper-ring-basalt, volcano-protocol

"Volcano Rx" is Kruse's name for living on, and grounding barefoot on, magnetic volcanic rock. In his model basalt holds a stable, flat magnetic field at the surface even when the planet's field weakens or tilts overhead, which makes volcanic regions refuges during the current decline. He ranks this above any device and offers passive stone and copper tools for people who cannot yet live there.

#### How basalt holds a flat field

Kruse starts from the rock itself. Lava from the mantle is rich in iron, and as it cools below about 550 °C, its Curie temperature (the point below which a mineral can hold magnetism), tiny crystals of magnetite and titanomagnetite form and line up like compass needles with the field of that moment. Frozen into basalt, they keep that direction permanently. More than 90% of volcanic rock is basalt, so large volcanoes and lava tubes create strong local magnetic anomalies.

Where the regional field is tipping toward vertical, Kruse says, this frozen magnetism bends the field lines flat and creates a horizontal micro-field at ground level, a "lithospheric straightener" that holds even if the agonic line moves or the core weakens. Grounding barefoot on basalt then supplies, in his account, a dense stream of earth-current electrons that charge the mitochondrial membrane, help ATP synthase (the rotary enzyme that makes the cell's energy currency) repel deuterium, or heavy hydrogen, and support melanin. Beside this he calls PEMF (pulsed electromagnetic field) devices the "loser option."

Not every volcanic place works. Hawaii's rock, he argues, froze in the direction of an earlier field, so it can cancel today's field instead of adding to it. Active volcanoes pin best, because every magnetic field needs moving charge; his own homes sit over water-filled lava tubes that he likens to a dynamo.

#### Why El Salvador

Kruse calls El Salvador a "geological ark." It lies near the magnetic equator (about 13°N), where the field already runs flat; its fault zone and volcanic chain run east to west, matching those flat lines; and deep below the surface lavas sit slowly cooled magma bodies full of very stable magnetite that act as a hidden anchor. Together, he says, these work as a "spatial shear deflector" that keeps a weakening field from tilting vertical. He adds that the country has sat near an agonic line (zero declination) for about 11,500 years, since the end of the Younger Dryas cold period, and that all of it qualifies because it is volcanic throughout. Elsewhere, iron-rich bedrock such as New York's Adirondacks offers a partial refuge.

#### Ancient builders

Kruse believes earlier cultures understood this. He reads the six tons of perforated magnetite and ilmenite blocks found at the Olmec site of San Lorenzo as a deliberate grid to restore a stable reference field when the dynamo sagged. Megaliths, dolmens and long barrows of granite, basalt and quartz-rich stone were, in his view, "paramagnetic sponges" (paramagnetic meaning weakly drawn into a magnetic field) that concentrate a weakening field inside the chamber like a lens. Built on earth-current lines and faults, their quartz was squeezed by tidal stress into making small currents, and people sheltered or were buried in such stone during past excursions.

#### Passive anchors: stone and copper

For people not yet on volcanic ground, Kruse prefers passive tools to powered stimulators that push "harsh, non-native" current into tissue. While grounded, rest a smooth basalt stone or volcanic clay behind the ear or on the temple, which he calls a "zero-frequency amplifier," or keep a paramagnetic stone in the pillow. Another option is an open copper ring about 30 cm (12 inches) across worn around the head or neck. He traces it to Georges Lakhovsky, who in 1925 at the Salpêtrière Hospital in Paris placed a single open copper ring around a geranium infected with tumor-causing bacteria; in Kruse's account that plant recovered while the untreated plants died. He also credits Slim Spurling's copper coils.

In his model such rings are passive antennas for the Schumann resonance (the natural 7.83 Hz electromagnetic hum between the ground and the upper atmosphere) and for geomagnetic waves. They raise cell voltage, help cells shed heavy isotopes, and let copper stand in for melanin where it is thin. The neck is his preferred target, his "vagal vortex," where the carotid arteries, the vagus nerve and structured water run together.

In practice, the barefoot grounding protocol says to ground on basalt or wet volcanic black sand. The El Salvador guide lists his preferred spots near active volcanoes such as Ilopango and says to test any lot with mosquitoes and candles before buying. The basalt-and-copper-ring protocol covers the stone, ring and pillow options, which he frames as a bridge while you fix your location. His Volcano Protocol copies a volcano's answer at body scale, pairing life in a pinned, low-latitude place and sunrise grounding on volcanic ground with steps to shake deuterium from the spine and build up carbon dioxide.

### Choosing a place to live
- **id:** choosing-a-location
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** Kruse calls location the final arbiter of mitochondrial health and gives three field numbers, a way to read the declination map and on-the-ground tests for choosing it.
- **protocols:** relocation-checklist, el-salvador-guide

For Kruse, where you live finally decides how well your mitochondria can run: no supplement, diet or exercise plan can outrun land that has lost its magnetic footing. He gives three numbers to look up, a way to read the declination map and tests for a specific lot, and he says your location sets how hard you must work every day to stay well.

#### The three numbers

His checklist uses the World Magnetic Model, the official field map. Total intensity, the field's strength in nanotesla (nT), should be as high as possible: above about 45,000 nT, ideally up to 60,000. Inclination, the angle at which field lines dip into the ground, should be 0–45°; flat lines, he says, anchor the body's internal axes, while lines plunging steeply toward the poles destabilize them. Declination, the angle between compass north and true north, should be close to 0°. A line of zero declination is called an agonic line, and on it there is no twist between the planet's spin axis and its field. He also says to avoid the South Atlantic Anomaly, a huge weak patch where intensity falls below about 22,000 nT and which, he says, now reaches the Caribbean.

The best places, in his model, are where an agonic line crosses a volcanic chain at a latitude with strong sun: his "fountain of youth coordinate." He notes that the North American agonic line runs down through the Midwest toward the Gulf and lines up with the Pacific's volcanoes, but the Midwest itself lacks volcanic ground. Agonic lines move with the poles, so he says to follow them rather than latitude, and that the best places will never be fixed; there are "no islands or oases."

#### Reading the maps

In his reading of the declination map, green (the agonic line) is best, red (east declination) acceptable and bold dark blue (strong west declination) very bad. Where the lines bunch together the field changes over short distances, which in his view shifts the alignment of mitochondrial membranes as you move around; that alone is a reason to leave. He compares his town's ESA Swarm satellite map on the same date across years to see which way it is trending.

Deserts give strong light but a weak magnetic background, and dry sand gives no ground, so he advises against raising children there. Some still-green land, he adds, already carries a desert-grade weak field; he names North America's breadbasket.

#### Testing a lot

Before buying land, Kruse reads what lives there. Mosquitoes active by day as well as night, a 24-hour beeswax candle that burns out early and a faltering local water cycle all warn of a weak spot; he used mosquitoes and candles to choose his own property. A "smart" house full of wireless devices, he says, cancels even a good location.

#### Where he says no, and where he says yes

He rules out California, which he says has a "double deficit" of high declination (about +11–12°) and too little steady UV-B sunlight, and calls the West Coast and Texas "cooked," with the East Coast next. He adds Florida, the Louisiana coast, Tennessee, Kansas, Colorado (a mile up, closer to the radiation belts), Hawaii, Minnesota (a decent but falling field and only about three months of useful sun), the UK, Germany, Mexico's Yucatán and Australia. In the US he names only the Pacific Northwest. His own choice is El Salvador's volcanic coast, though not every spot there is equal; he avoids crowded, wireless-heavy beaches such as El Zonte.

#### After the move

Kruse warns that people can feel worse after moving somewhere better. Borrowing Robert Becker's idea of "adaptive healing," he says tissue has to rebuild for the new environment, much as running modern current through 1920s wiring can burn a house down. People of northern descent, he adds, should adapt to tropical sun gradually.

In practice, the relocation checklist gathers his criteria: the three World Magnetic Model numbers, avoiding the South Atlantic Anomaly, reading declination and Swarm maps, grounding on basalt or wet black sand, testing lots with mosquitoes, candles and the water cycle, no smart house, and ground-floor living. The El Salvador guide adds his local notes: buy an empty lot and build, favor areas near active volcanoes, and use the Costa del Sol beach for sunrise grounding.

### Haplogroup and the "magnetic zipcode"
- **id:** haplogroup-zipcode
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** Kruse says your maternal mitochondrial lineage is the blueprint that decides which local field suits you, and a mismatch speeds aging.
- **protocols:** four-phase-sun-day, relocation-checklist

A haplogroup is a maternal lineage traced through mitochondrial DNA, which passes from mother to child. Kruse calls it the blueprint and your "magnetic zipcode," the field where you live, the dial. When the two don't match, he argues, aging speeds up, so the same place can suit one person and harm another.

He frames it as a chain: the haplogroup is the blueprint, Marcus theory (the physics of how fast electrons jump between molecules) is the law, the magnetic zipcode is the dial, and disease is the failure that follows a mismatch. Lineages arose, he says, as answers to past changes in Earth's field. As people left Africa for colder climates, the M and N lineages picked up changes that let some of the mitochondria's energy leak away as heat instead of being stored, a trait called "uncoupling" that helps in the cold.

#### Different tolerances

Kruse ranks lineages by how they cope as the field weakens. African L lineages, especially L1, evolved near the equator, where the field is flat and naturally weak, so in his model they function with little magnetic help. Declination is a separate matter: for an L lineage, he says, 2–4° of offset between compass and true north can be enough for a stage 4 cancer.

The European H lineage, his own, is "leaky": when magnetic pinning slips it can drop its voltage and shed the surplus as heat, which he says clears deuterium and resets the cell. East and Southeast Asian D5b1 and F lineages are the most vulnerable. They stayed where strong sun and the dense magnetic pinning of the Pacific "Ring of Fire" volcanoes were constant, so they have neither L1's low-field tolerance nor H's heat release. Lineages that crossed the Bering region (D1–D4, A, B and C) were, by contrast, hardened by intense high-latitude conditions.

For D5b1 and F in a weak field he says cold therapy backfires. They should avoid deep cold (water around 55–65 °F) until they are magnetically pinned, first maximizing morning UV and infrared light, grounding fully and shielding strictly from wireless fields.

#### Northern bodies and the field

Kruse says northern peoples shed skin melanin, the pigment he treats as a spin filter, and leaned on strong polar field lines instead. He calls this "Zeeman outsourcing," after the Zeeman effect by which a magnetic field splits electron energy levels. A strong polar field plus cold, he argues, let them grow larger, using the body as a bigger solar panel. Coming out of the Laschamp excursion about 41,000 years ago, he says, humans had smaller, wetter, more melanin-rich brains. He also reads Weston A. Price's famous survey of traditional diets as a map of local magnetic conditions rather than a list of recipes.

#### Moving to the tropics

A northern lineage moving into strong tropical sun must, in his view, adapt gradually. A sudden plunge into equatorial UV can overwhelm a leaky H mitochondrion, while stepping through the day's light and grounding on basalt or wet black sand builds a "solar callus," skin and tissue adapted to strong sun, without changing DNA. Redness, he says, means you pushed too far.

In practice, the four-phase sun day protocol lays out that schedule: sunrise red and infrared light with feet on basalt or wet black sand, mid-morning UV-A, a few minutes of midday UV-B that grows slowly as you tan, and late-day sun, never to the point of redness. The relocation checklist reminds you that your maternal haplogroup sets how much declination you can tolerate.

### The South Atlantic Anomaly, muons and failing regions
- **id:** magnetosphere-saa-deuterium
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** Kruse says Earth's weakening field, worst under the expanding South Atlantic Anomaly, lets in particles that load the body with deuterium, and names the regions he sees failing.
- **protocols:** track-your-field

Kruse argues that Earth's magnetic shield is weakening and that the damage is worst under the South Atlantic Anomaly, a huge and growing patch where the field is weakest. In his model the thinner shield lets in particles that load bodies, water and food with heavy hydrogen, and he names the regions he expects to fail first.

#### Failing shields

Kruse says three "cosmic shields" are failing together: a filter of interstellar hydrogen around the solar system, the heliosphere (the Sun's protective bubble) and the geodynamo (the field made by Earth's core). He puts the loss so far at about 10% of field strength, falling roughly 5% a decade, and ties rising epidemics to the years since 1859, when the magnetic pole began moving fast, with a sharp rise after 1940. Another 10–30% drop would, he says, be an evolutionary bottleneck, and his main worry is a 30–50% loss within 50 years.

#### The anomaly

The South Atlantic Anomaly (SAA) is where the shield is thinnest, below about 22,000 nT against the 45,000 nT or more he looks for. Citing ESA's Swarm satellites, he says it has split into two lobes: the main low drifting west over South America, deepest near Asunción in Paraguay and Foz do Iguaçu in Brazil, and a second stretching toward southwest Africa and Cape Town. Its northern edge now reaches the Lesser Antilles, including Dominica, which is why he treats the Caribbean as off-limits.

#### Muons and deuterium

His key study is a paper by Augusto and colleagues. Under the anomaly the "cutoff rigidity," the minimum energy a cosmic particle needs to get through the field, collapses, and muons (heavy cousins of electrons made when cosmic rays strike the air) rise up to tenfold at ground level. He reads this as the Van Allen radiation belts sagging closer to the surface. Muons pass through walls and tissue, and in his model act as positive "dopants" that cancel the negative charge of cell membranes and knock light hydrogen out of its channels, so deuterium (heavy hydrogen) builds up in the basal ganglia, heart and liver.

The lower belts also, he says, change how moisture is ionized in the air, raising deuterium in rain and so in grass and groundwater, and water's deuterium content will swing more widely as the dynamo fails. Cosmic neutrons striking nitrogen make tritium, the radioactive form of hydrogen, which rains onto South American farms and rivers. The body builds it into DNA as if it were ordinary hydrogen; when it decays into helium, which cannot bond, the chemical link snaps from the inside. For this reason he avoids food from the SAA, and he says seeds germinate poorly in its weak field.

#### Who fails first

Zip codes near the expanding anomaly, Kruse predicts, will fail first: early-onset Alzheimer's and Parkinson's, cardiomyopathy and arrhythmia, grade 4 cancers and shorter lives. Parkinson's in particular, he says, strikes where magnetic flux is weakest. He calls the West Coast and Texas "cooked" and the East Coast next; people there, he says, sense something has changed without being able to name it. California has a "double deficit" of high declination and too little steady UV-B sun. He adds Florida, which he says the change "swallowed" in about 14 years, and the Louisiana coast, says Australia is losing field strength locally, and names the Pacific Northwest as the only US option.

In practice, the track-your-field protocol says to watch declination drift, secular-acceleration ("jerk") charts and Swarm data for your own zip code, comparing the same date across years, along with unusual blocked weather and your own heart-rate variability. If your area shows a jerk or turns toward a vertical field, move before official charts catch up. He also keeps a watch-list for a bigger shift: field loss reaching 30–50% of the Laschamp collapse, sudden changes in the SAA and faster pole drift.

### Solar storms, the Schumann signal and the "thalamic GPS"
- **id:** solar-flares-space-weather
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** Kruse says the thalamus works as a position sensor locked to Earth's field and resonance, which solar storms and high declination knock out of alignment.
- **protocols:** space-weather-days, brain-eye-ear-conditions

Kruse argues that the brain keeps track of where the body is by locking onto Earth's magnetic field and its natural electromagnetic rhythm. Solar storms, and more slowly a skewed local field, knock that internal position sense out of alignment, which in his model explains storm headaches, a cluster of heart, ear and immune symptoms, and agoraphobia.

#### The Sun and the thalamic GPS

Kruse treats the Sun as one node on a galactic electrical circuit, with its 11-year cycle an induced response. In the body the receiver is the thalamus, the brain's relay center. He calls it a "thalamic GPS," a three-axis position panel whose axes he places along the sphenoid bone at the base of the skull, the vagus nerve and the heart. It locks onto the Schumann resonance, the 7.83 Hz electromagnetic hum of the space between the ground and the upper atmosphere, and onto the horizontal and vertical parts of the field. The brain's alpha rhythm, around 10 Hz, acts in his model like a strobe that keeps cell membranes aligned.

#### What a storm does

A solar flare or coronal mass ejection (CME, a burst of charged solar material) compresses the magnetosphere, the field's protective bubble, and drives an induced surge through the ground. Kruse says this jams the Schumann signal and uncouples the thalamic GPS, disturbing alpha waves. Cerebrospinal fluid, in his account, loses its charge, thickens and drains poorly, so pressure inside the skull rises; hence storm headaches. He reads glioma patients' storm-time seizures and brain swelling as field events rather than random inflammation, and tells members that a bad night can come from a CME rather than from what they ate. He calls earthquakes, such as two in Panama during a recent storm, detectors of magnetic anomalies.

He adds that the field also shifts over each ordinary day. Sunlight charges the upper atmosphere, whose currents nudge the ground field by roughly 10–100 nT; after sunset those currents fade and the field settles to a quieter night baseline. As an extreme storm he cites the 1859 Carrington Event, when auroras reached the Caribbean, and ties it to the collapse of European vineyards in the years that followed.

#### Slow uncoupling from the local field

High declination, Kruse says, does the same thing slowly. A field twisted relative to true north, or changing quickly, keeps the thalamus from locking a stable reference. That lowers tone in the right vagus nerve and makes mitochondria leak DNA, which the immune system treats as an alarm. His three signature symptoms of this uncoupling are heart palpitations, tinnitus and autoimmune flares.

#### Agoraphobia as a navigation failure

Kruse reframes agoraphobia as this navigation system failing rather than as a fear disorder. The brain's grid cells and place cells, which map position, rely in his model on structured water held in order by the field. ### The planet's story: excursions, the magnetic forge and volcanic CO₂
- **id:** geomagnetic-excursions-evolution
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** Kruse reads Earth's field history — excursions, ozone, oxygen and volcanoes — as the large-scale version of the physics that runs the cell.
- **protocols:** none

Kruse reads Earth's magnetic history as the large-scale version of the physics he sees inside cells. In his model, periods when the field steadied allowed complex life, periods when it faltered reshaped or culled it, and the weakening under way now is another such turning point: a planetary re-tuning rather than an extinction.

#### Excursions and reversals

A reversal is a full swap of the magnetic poles; it takes about 2,000–10,000 years and then holds for 100,000 years or more. An excursion is an aborted reversal: the field weakens and wanders for a few centuries up to about 2,000 years, then recovers. The Laschamp excursion about 41,000 years ago and the Mono Lake excursion are examples. Seafloor magnetic stripes smooth excursions out, Kruse notes, but fast-settling lake and deep-sea sediments record hundreds of them, along with spikes in beryllium-10 and carbon-14 that mark extra cosmic rays. Reversals, in his view, cause extinctions because ozone is stripped for thousands of years; excursions select survivors.

#### A steady field made complex life

Kruse argues that complex life arrived only once Earth's dynamo steadied. During the "Boring Billion," the long, quiet middle of Earth's history, a weak and unstable field let the solar wind strip ozone, leaving the surface scorched by UV; he cites modelling by Cooke and colleagues that puts ozone below 120 Dobson units when oxygen was 1% of today's level. Oxygen, he says, kept flipping into its reactive singlet form. The Cambrian explosion, when about 32 animal body plans appeared, began in his account when the inner core hardened, the field stabilized and oxygen locked into its stable triplet state, letting one-handed molecules such as collagen and melanin act as spin filters. He argues that Nick Lane's oxygen-centered explanation misses this, citing 2025–2026 genomic studies in *Nature* (publicized by NIOZ and the University of Copenhagen) showing that complex-cell machinery arose before oxygen was abundant.

#### Laschamp and modern humans

At Laschamp, Kruse says, the field fell below 5% of normal and auroras reached the equator. He links the event to the "chromosome 2 fuse," the spot where two ancestral ape chromosomes fused in humans, which sits beside genes for melanin, fetal hemoglobin and metabolism; in his model it tripped and reset human metabolism. Neanderthals, he says, "short-circuited" rather than lost a war, while modern humans emerged with a larger melanin shield and a burst of cognition and cave art. He also ties excursions to collagen failure, pointing to elongated skulls such as Peru's Paracas infants, and calls today's rise in joint hypermobility a planetary effect.

#### The magnetic forge today

Kruse frames current changes as planetary rebalancing, not something humans caused. Earth, he says, is leaving a sunlight-dominated era for a "magnetic forge" like Laschamp's. He puts the present at about 9% of a Laschamp-scale collapse; at 30–50% he expects Earth's core and outer shell to decouple, his "Tau" stage. He cites Madsen and colleagues (University of Edinburgh, 2026), who used Swarm and other satellite data to show that molten iron under the equatorial Pacific switched from westward to eastward flow in 2010 and has weakened since about 2020. He follows the ECDO theory, which proposes a sudden slip of Earth's outer shell, as a staged cycle he believes has begun.

#### Oceans, weather and volcanoes

Moving, salty ocean currents, the Atlantic and Southern overturning circulations (AMOC and SMOC), make their own magnetic fields and, in his model, are anchored to the dynamo. As they slow, he reads stalled currents, a silent Atlantic hurricane season, a hyperactive Pacific and harder El Niño "sloshing" as magnetic effects; a collapsing AMOC, he says, keeps loading people in Florida with deuterium. Warming seas release iodine, which destroys ozone far faster than chlorine, and volcanoes answer with CO₂ that dissolves as bicarbonate and locks the iodine back into the water. He welcomes this countermeasure and says humans need more CO₂, not less, during an excursion. Finally, he maps planets onto cell states in his "Universal Scaling Law" (gas giants healthy, Earth stressed, Venus cancerous, Mars necrotic), so colonizing Mars, he says, would be lethal.

In practice, Kruse treats this history as a reason to act rather than despair. Those who anchor themselves to a stable field, protect their internal water, build melanin with sunlight and escape blue-lit indoor life will, he says, carry humanity through the transition. He rejects "balance" or moderation during an excursion, and the planet-scale story is why he puts location ahead of every other health choice.

### nnEMF: wireless, wiring and the "hyper-local excursion"

In the blood, a rapidly shifting potassium level means, to Kruse, that the nearest agonic line (where compass north and true north agree) is moving away or that the person is spoiling their own magnetic tracking.
