## Light

### Sunrise: dawn UV-A and the master clock
- **id:** sunrise-why
- **pillar:** light
- **summary:** Kruse says dawn light — UV-A near 380 nm plus red and near-infrared, taken in through bare eyes and skin — is the master signal that sets the body's clocks and readies its energy machinery for the day.
- **protocols:** sunrise-horizon

Kruse argues that the most important light of the day is the first light at sunrise. In his model its UV-A (the gentle ultraviolet just beyond visible violet, which he pins near 380 nanometres), together with the red and near-infrared that dominate the low sun, is the master signal that tells every clock in the body what time it is. Because he treats mitochondria, the cell's power plants, as light-driven devices, missing sunrise means starting the day with the body's wiring out of step.

#### How light reaches the clock

Kruse says most of light's effect on the body has nothing to do with seeing. Citing the ophthalmologist Fritz Hollwich's 1948 work on the "energetic portion" of the visual pathway, along with Hufeland, Paracelsus and Franz Halberg's early circadian research, he describes an "optico-magnetic" route: light entering the eyes and skin steers the hypothalamus and pituitary, the brain's hormone control centre. The receivers are not the cells used for vision but other light-sensitive materials — neuropsin, melanopsin, melanin, cholesterol, water, heme and collagen.

Neuropsin (also called OPN5) is the one he puts first. It is a UV-A sensor in the eye and skin, and in his account 380 nm light striking it sets the daily rhythm of NAD⁺, the molecule cells use to carry electrons and to run repair enzymes such as SIRT1. That keeps Complex I, the entry point of the mitochondrial energy chain, working cleanly. He calls 380 nm the "mTOR switch" because it tells mTOR, the cell's build-or-clean-up regulator, which mode to be in. He also treats NAD⁺ itself as a light-storing material that relays light to Complex I.

Kruse describes neuropsin as a "dual-axis transistor": it reads sunlight on one axis and Earth's magnetic field on the other. When the two agree — his "Zeeman handshake" — oxygen stays in its stable, safe form (triplet oxygen) and metabolism flows toward repair. When the field is disturbed or the light is artificial, he says the handshake fails and the same chemistry tips toward histamine, inflammation and singlet oxygen, oxygen's reactive and damaging form.

#### What sunrise renews

In his model, sunrise UV-A "renovates" heme (the iron-holding core of hemoglobin and of the energy chain's cytochromes) and melanin. The mild stress of UV-A switches on POMC, a hormone precursor the body cuts into alpha-MSH, the signal that makes melanin; mitochondria make melatonin at the same time to buffer that stress. Because the first step of heme production happens inside mitochondria and depends on UV, he links many anemias to missing full-spectrum sun. The same light, he adds, gives hair its colour.

Kruse also says the body reads sunlight as a sentence, in order. The blue pre-dawn hour is the preface that anchors the clock, the amber sunrise is the opening line, and dusk is the closing chapter that cues night-time melatonin and repair. A lone orange LED switched on at a random hour carries none of that meaning. He points to Egypt and the Maya at Tikal as cultures that organised their days around this sequence.

The first horizon light, he says, also triggers the cortisol awakening response (the natural morning rise in cortisol) and the glucagon gene network. Together these start the pancreas's roughly two-litre daily bicarbonate flush, which in his model sweeps deuterium (heavy hydrogen) out through the gut. Morning sun also lets the longevity gene FOXO3 apply its brakes on cell division, which he says protects the genome.

#### In practice

His sunrise-horizon routine is to be outside as the sun clears the horizon, using the first 60–90 minutes and staying 30–45 minutes. Nothing should sit between your eyes and the sun: no sunglasses, contacts, glasses or window glass. Uncover as much skin as you reasonably can and stand barefoot on surf, wet sand, dewy grass or basalt. He takes a well-formed stool (Bristol type 4) as the sign that the morning bicarbonate flush is working.

### Midday UV-B and vitamin D
- **id:** solar-noon-vitamin-d
- **pillar:** light
- **summary:** In Kruse's model, UV-B and the vitamin D it makes in skin are part of a light-and-magnetic-field system with a built-in safety cutoff that pills bypass, and the local magnetic field limits how much vitamin D sunlight can make.
- **protocols:** sun-exposure, pills-he-avoids

Kruse rejects the idea that vitamin D is just a nutrient to top up. In his view UV-B, the shorter and stronger ultraviolet of the midday sun, and the vitamin D it makes in skin form a self-regulating light system tied to the body clock and to the local magnetic field. That is why he wants vitamin D made in the skin, not swallowed.

#### UV-B as a safety brake

Kruse treats UV-B and UV-A as a pair of brakes on the mitochondrial energy chain. Vitamin D's receptor, which he places on the inner mitochondrial membrane, slows electron flow between Complexes III and IV, while UV-A releases nitric oxide, which briefly slows Complex IV. Together, he says, they keep the chain out of "Marcus inversion" — his term for electron flow that stalls when it is pushed too hard — whenever Earth's field is unstable.

That is why he calls UV-B "term life insurance" for anyone who is not "pinned" (living grounded and sunlit under a stable local magnetic field), and why he says it should not be feared even by people with cancer. Citing Slominski, Holick and Reiter (2026), he treats vitamin D and the circadian clock as one coupled loop. He adds that mitochondria make melatonin in daytime using UV, pointing to melatonin's absorption peaks near 224 and 290 nm, so vitamin D and melatonin are twin products of the sun rather than day-and-night opposites.

#### The built-in cutoff

UV-B turns a cholesterol precursor in the skin into vitamin D, but Kruse notes that only about a quarter of the yield becomes vitamin D3; the rest becomes lumisterol and tachysterol. He calls this a self-limiting "thermostat" that makes it impossible to overdose on sun-made vitamin D. In his account the UV-B hit creates a radical pair — two fragments each holding one unpaired electron. If their spins point opposite ways they recombine into D3; if they point the same way they break down into the cutoff products. The local magnetic field tips that balance.

Because the field chooses the path, he says the magnetism where you live caps how much vitamin D you can make — his explanation for why levels may not rise in California despite plenty of sun. The form of skin cholesterol matters too. In summer, sulfur-rich cholesterol carried in HDL is the "antenna" tuned to UV-B. Years of blue light and nnEMF (non-native electromagnetic fields from wireless and wiring) can freeze skin cholesterol in its electron-poor LDL form, his "Mismatch Freeze," so even naked equatorial sun finds nothing to work with. A cell loaded with deuterium (heavy hydrogen), he adds, lacks the energy to make or activate vitamin D at all.

#### More sun is not always better

Strong UV without grounding and infrared can itself stall the energy chain, so Kruse reads a sunburn as proof the person was not pinned enough, and calls burning on purpose foolish. Citing Fosbury's maps of US deaths, he notes that deaths from heart disease, cancer, stroke, diabetes, Alzheimer's and flu all peak one to three weeks after the winter solstice and bottom out after the summer solstice; he blames low light combined with an unstable field.

#### Why pills fall short

An oral pill delivers pure D3 with none of the cutoff products and none of the light-run steps. Kruse cites an observational study of more than 5,000 older adults, reported in the Washington Post, that linked routine supplementation in people with normal levels to slightly faster cognitive decline. In his reading the extra vitamin D acts as an "atomic impurity" in an ungrounded brain under artificial light. He treats a low blood level as a sign of melanin breakdown, not a reason to supplement.

#### In practice

His sun-exposure routine: near solar noon, when your shadow is shorter than you, expose your torso and back, with no sunscreen, sunglasses, contacts or glasses. Build up gradually and move to shade at the first warmth on the skin. Give UV-B priority if you are not pinned and around the winter solstice, and stay grounded while you do it. Oral vitamin D3, especially when levels are already normal, is on his list of pills to avoid.

### The four-phase sun day and the "solar callus"
- **id:** solar-callus
- **pillar:** light
- **summary:** Kruse's account of how a person, especially one from a northern maternal lineage, builds tolerance to strong sun by stepping through the day's wavelengths from sunrise to sunset.
- **protocols:** four-phase-sun-day, sunset-viewing

Kruse uses "solar callus" for skin and tissue that have adapted to strong sun, so they tan rather than burn. He argues the callus is built gradually, not inherited, and that how it is built matters most for someone whose ancestors evolved far from the equator and who moves to the tropics, as he did when he moved to El Salvador.

#### Lineage and pace

He explains this through haplogroups, the maternal lineages traced through mitochondrial DNA. His own, H, is northern European, and he describes its mitochondria as naturally "leaky": built to shed heat rather than to couple tightly to energy production. Plunged suddenly into tropical UV, he says, such mitochondria lose their coherence. An H-lineage person who flies straight from northern Europe to San Salvador is, in his view, in a very different position from one who arrives slowly.

Mitochondrial DNA does not change, and a northern lineage does not become a tropical one. But Kruse says epigenetic flexibility plus local grounding — bare feet on basalt or wet black volcanic sand, which he says carry a strong horizontal magnetic field — lets the person "re-couple." The mitochondria become better insulated, leak less, and keep the blood and spinal-fluid flows that clear deuterium (heavy hydrogen) working. He calls this a re-coupled relocation.

#### Four phases of light

He built his own callus by treating each day as a training program through the solar spectrum, in order. Sunrise red and near-infrared expand structured water in tissue (water ordered into a charge-holding gel layer) and prepare cells to handle the UV that comes later. Mid-morning UV-A on bare eyes signals the brain, through the POMC hormone pathway, to lay down melanin, which he treats as the skin's spin filter. A brief midday UV-B dose makes vitamin D and sulfur-rich cholesterol and, together with the new melanin, completes the callus. The last daylight hour's red and infrared then repair the day's oxidative damage and prepare the body to make melatonin. In his model the infrared at both ends prepares and repairs the tissue around the UV-heavy middle.

#### Redness and filters

Redness, Kruse says, means the membrane's electrical containment has broken down, so his rule is never to burn. Sunscreen blocks the UV-B and UV-A needed for vitamin D and melanin. Sunglasses make the brain read cloud or darkness, so the melanin signal never starts and the person burns quickly at midday; contacts interfere in the same way. He names DHA from wild seafood as the fat that turns sunlight into current in tissue, and says saffron speeds the callus: its carotenoids crocin and crocetin, its aroma compound safranal and the flavonol kaempferol.

#### Skin colour as a readout

In Kruse's view pale skin is not a sign of health but an atrophied organ, made pale by sunscreen, sunglasses, indoor life or drugs. Near-infrared sun draws blood to the skin and boosts red-cell production, and strong UV keeps blood oxygen in its stable form, turning blood deep red. Sun-flushed skin is, for him, a visible readout of redox health that other species also use to choose mates (he cites gelada baboons and birds). He retells accounts, which he credits to Lavoisier, of ship surgeons near the equator mistaking bright-scarlet venous blood for arterial blood.

#### In practice

His four-phase sun day: at sunrise, 30–45 minutes of red and near-infrared on bare eyes and as much skin as possible, feet on basalt or wet black sand; mid-morning (about 8:30–11:00), 15–25 minutes of UV-A on eyes and skin while moving, with no eyewear; at midday, when your shadow is shorter than you, 3–10 minutes of UV-B on torso and back while grounded, starting at 3 minutes and adding a minute every few days as you tan, and moving to shade at the first warmth. In the last daylight hour, spend 20–30 minutes outside with bare skin and no sunglasses to wind down (his sunset-viewing practice). Keep artificial night light and out-of-season food out of the picture, and if you are relocating to the tropics, arrive gradually.

### Red and near-infrared: the sun first
- **id:** red-nir-light
- **pillar:** light
- **summary:** Kruse values red and near-infrared light highly but says it cannot fix an unpinned body on its own, and that the sun is its only complete source.
- **protocols:** red-nir-light

Red and near-infrared light (NIR — invisible light just past red, roughly 600–1000 nm) hold a central place in Kruse's model. About 43% of sunlight falls in this band, he says, and it reaches deep into the body to power mitochondria. But he insists it is only one part of what sunlight provides, and that it only works fully in a body that is "pinned": grounded and sunlit under a stable local magnetic field.

#### What red and infrared do

Building on Peter Mitchell's account of how mitochondria make energy, Kruse calls red light the sun's "command channel" for light hydrogen (ordinary hydrogen, as opposed to heavy deuterium). Red light, he says, penetrates the abdomen to the liver's mitochondria and spins ATP synthase — the rotary motor that makes ATP, the cell's energy currency — at full efficiency on light hydrogen. The liver then needs less food fuel and keeps deuterium out; he calls this the "43% red light engine." He puts the energy barrier for smooth electron and proton flow down the energy chain at about 0.66 electron-volts, which he says matches near-infrared photons. He also describes Complex III's recycling loop (the Q-cycle) as a "deli slicer" that shaves light frequencies off the electrons food supplies.

NIR's best-known job in his model is at Complex IV, the last step of the energy chain, where oxygen is turned into water. Nitric oxide can sit on Complex IV's copper–iron centre and block it. Morning NIR breaks that bond — his "photonic purge" — so oxygen can enter. Without it, he says, electrons pile up, oxygen flips into its reactive singlet form, and the cell can no longer make its own deuterium-depleted water.

#### Near-Infrared Deficiency Syndrome

Kruse coined "Near-Infrared Deficiency Syndrome" (NIRDS) for the modern lack of 600–1000 nm light. An ancestor sitting by a wood fire, in the sun and grounded to a strong local field, was bathed in it. Modern people live under LEDs that emit almost none, and the phase-out of incandescent bulbs removed the last indoor source.

#### Why NIR alone is not enough

Yet Kruse says NIR "lubricates rather than drives." It thins the water around ATP synthase and speeds Complex IV, but it does nothing to align electron spins, the job his model gives to the magnetic field. If Complexes I–III upstream are misaligned by a weak field or nnEMF (non-native electromagnetic fields from wireless and wiring), speeding up Complex IV only deepens the traffic jam and risks a heat dump; he likens it to flooring the accelerator with a crushed transmission. He calls NIR an excellent tool but says relying on it alone, as some near-infrared teachers do (he names Fosbury), breaks nature's rules: evolution added UV-A and UV-B sensing as a safety margin for a planet whose field varies.

He goes further for people who are unpinned. When oxygen is in its singlet state and NAD⁺ is loaded with deuterium, he says metabolism becomes blind to light, so magnetism now matters more than light — a catch-22 he calls rare in human history. Such a person can have NIRDS while standing naked in the sun. The precondition for using light is what he calls a "paramagnetic lock": membranes, cholesterol rafts and structured water acting together as an ordered antenna that keeps oxygen in its stable form.

Kruse also warns against a popular combination. Methylene blue shuttles electrons past Complexes I and III, and 670 or 830 nm light excites Complex IV; in an ungrounded person living under wireless and blue light, he likens the pairing to a turbocharger on a short-circuiting transformer and links it to testicular and ovarian cancer.

#### In practice

His red-nir-light guidance puts the sun first: get red and infrared from sunrise and daylight, and if you have hours of sun, a panel is redundant. In the tropics he skips light devices altogether. Don't rely on single-wavelength devices, which he says can harm; where he does use panels, they are 660 and 850 nm. For problems such as eye swelling he recommends grounding plus solar UV-A and near-infrared rather than panels, and he says not to combine methylene blue with red light unless grounded and away from wireless and blue light.

### Melanin: the body's solar semiconductor
- **id:** melanin-semiconductor
- **pillar:** light
- **summary:** Kruse treats melanin as a light-driven semiconductor and metal trap that daily UV-A must renew, and sees its breakdown as a root of many diseases.
- **protocols:** sun-exposure

For Kruse, melanin is far more than skin pigment. Found in skin, eyes, brain, the nerves around the heart, fat and fascia, it is in his model a "biological quantum semiconductor" — a material that turns light into electrical current — and a vault for metals. Keeping it renewed with daily sunlight is central to his approach, and he sees its breakdown beneath a long list of diseases.

#### What melanin does

Kruse compares melanin to chlorophyll. It absorbs a broad range of light, especially UV and visible, and uses it to split water into hydrogen, oxygen and free electrons. That continual splitting feeds a "wireless" current into cells that does not depend on food. Working with structured water (water ordered into a charge-holding layer), he says, melanin also sorts light hydrogen from deuterium, its heavy form.

Melanin is also a trap and a buffer. Its chemical groups grab free iron and copper, which in his account stops the Fenton reaction (iron-driven chemistry that makes destructive radicals) and singlet-oxygen formation at the source. It buffers protons, magnesium and calcium like a capacitor (a store that takes up and releases charge), steadying the conditions mitochondria need to handle calcium. Because it is built from L-DOPA and dopamine, it soaks up excess dopamine before it can oxidise. During light famines in Earth's history, he says, dense melanin also locked away manganese and rare-earth lanthanides.

In the brain, Kruse calls neuromelanin bound to iron in the substantia nigra a solid-state battery that holds local magnetic fields steady so incoming oxygen keeps its stable spin; nnEMF, blue light or dehydration deform it. A skin tan, he says, shows only the outer shield, and this "interior tan" matters more.

#### Renewal, and how melanin "rusts"

Melanin must be renewed. Without 380 nm UV-A reaching neuropsin (a UV-A sensor in eye and skin) and without strong "pinning" (living grounded and sunlit under a stable local field), the POMC pathway — the hormone precursor the body cuts into alpha-MSH, the signal that makes melanin — shuts down. The ageing polymer then soaks up amine toxins, chemicals and deuterium, its iron "rusts" to the oxidised ferric form, and it releases free iron and oxidised dopamine. The debris settles as lipofuscin, his "rusted melanin," which he says clogs the brainstem's sleep centres. Degraded melanin also feeds a breakdown route toward quinolinic acid that silences BDNF, the brain's growth signal.

Kruse reads a low vitamin D level as a stand-in for this melanin breakdown, not as a reason to supplement. Greying hair, he says, is a visible readout of the same circuit (the MITF pathway that controls pigment cells), and once follicles repigment they again turn light into structured water.

#### What blocks the signal

Blocking daylight, in his model, cuts the eye-to-skin signal that builds melanin. Sunglasses make the brain read darkness, so pigment is never ordered and the person burns at midday; contacts, sunscreen and daytime blue blockers do the same. Moderate tanning ability, he adds, signals melanin potential that sunlight and grounding can put to work.

#### Skin water and lotions

Kruse treats dry skin as a light-clock failure, not a shortage of drinking water. Light at night disrupts the POMC signal, and the eccrine sweat glands — which he describes as water-sorting organs wired to the melanin loop — stop building a film of structured, deuterium-depleted water on the skin and leak ordinary water instead. Ancestral skin, he says, made its own lipid-and-water film that turned UV into energy, so no lotions were needed; skin is hydrated from the inside, by mitochondria making water.

Lotions, in his view, make matters worse. Any layer on the skin changes the light that reaches it. Petroleum and silicone bases collapse skin water from its structured state (his "160") to ordinary water (his "78"); hidden UV filters breed singlet oxygen under LEDs; and, citing 2025 research from the Max Planck Institute for Chemistry, any synthetic coat smothers the "oxidation field" of hydroxyl radicals the skin makes at its surface. A single natural fat such as tallow or coconut oil is his alternative.

#### Melanin in deep time

Kruse also gives melanin a role in surviving planetary crises. Melanin loaded with lanthanides, he says, turns radiation into a direct current, letting melanised life "carry its own sun" through long darkness. That is his explanation for why bird and mammal ancestors survived the ash-darkened world after the K–T impact while dinosaurs, dependent on outside sunlight, died.

#### In practice

His sun-exposure routine is the main lever: regular unfiltered sun, with sunrise UV-A for renewal, and no sunscreen, sunglasses, contacts or blue blockers in daylight. For dry skin he uses morning sun, cold and grounding rather than lotion; after a shower he allows at most tallow or coconut oil, and never petroleum, silicone or UV-filter products.

### Blue light, LEDs and screens
- **id:** blue-light-hygiene
- **pillar:** light
- **summary:** Kruse blames blue-dominant indoor light and screens, stripped of red and infrared, for damage to the body clock and metabolism, and calls screen life "reality subtraction."
- **protocols:** blue-light-hygiene, screen-light-hygiene

Kruse argues that modern indoor light is one of the biggest drivers of chronic disease — in his view a bigger one than food. LEDs and screens deliver a spike of blue light with almost none of the red and infrared that come with natural light, and people take it in indoors, away from both the sun and the ground.

#### How lighting changed

He dates a turning point to efficient blue LEDs, whose inventors (Akasaki, Amano and Nakamura) won the 2014 physics Nobel. Built to emit only visible light, LEDs strip out the red and infrared that sunlight, firelight and incandescent bulbs supply — the longer wavelengths that, in Kruse's model, penetrate the body and power mitochondria. Phasing out incandescent bulbs removed the last indoor source of infrared.

#### What it does to the body

Indoor spaces, Kruse says, are both blue-dominant and "devoid of magnetic effects." That combination forces a spin change in the mitochondrial energy chain (intersystem crossing) that drives insulin spikes and insulin resistance; he holds that light and missing magnetic "pinning" raise blood sugar and insulin more than any food. A burst of red light while grounded, he adds, can blunt a glucose and insulin spike.

Wrong-way light also makes singlet oxygen, an energised form of oxygen that bypasses the body's normal safety controls. Kruse calls it a "molecular buzzsaw": it burns the fatty membrane rafts that anchor PER2, a core clock protein, which he names as the root of every circadian mismatch. In his model the body clock is physical hardware being damaged from inside, not an abstract genetic timer.

The eye takes the first hit. He says nnEMF (non-native electromagnetic fields from wireless and wiring), blue light and a weak local field damage melanopsin (the eye's blue-sensing clock receptor), mitochondrial DNA and heme proteins, dry out the retina's melanin and let deuterium (heavy hydrogen) into the eye's water. Contact lenses worn around the clock, he adds, trap blue light and EMF right at the eye.

#### When to block blue — and when not to

After sunset, blue light should be gone. But Kruse is equally firm that blue blockers of any tint, red or yellow included, should never be worn in daylight. There, he says, they act on the clock "like a drug": they change the body's own feedback loops in the same way a supplement or medication does, and they block the daylight signals that build melanin.

#### Screens and "reality subtraction"

Kruse calls social media and AI feeds "reality subtraction": algorithms that replace direct perception of the real world with a profit-driven version, exploiting dopamine while users sit ungrounded under blue light. The weaker the magnetic field a person senses, he says, the more marketing and manipulation get through. Screens also spoil exercise. In his account blue light from TV leaves the clock genes and DNA weighed down, and training on top of that only multiplies the strain, so going straight from the television to a workout makes exercise partly toxic; he points to the hip replacements of ancestral-health author Mark Sisson as an example.

#### In practice

His blue-light-hygiene rules: swap LED and fluorescent bulbs for incandescent; after sunset, use red incandescent bulbs or glasses that block 100% of blue; never wear blue blockers in daylight; and by day, open a window rather than buy sun-imitating lamps. His screen-light-hygiene rules: cut screen and wireless time, don't go straight from TV or screens into a workout, and keep devices out of the bedroom.

## Water

### Deuterium and what to drink
- **id:** deuterium-and-water
- **pillar:** water
- **summary:** Kruse says healthy mitochondria make their own deuterium-depleted water, that deuterium can only be excreted, and that it matters most once magnetic or electrical interference has already damaged the cell's engine.
- **protocols:** drinking-water, ddw-targets

Deuterium is hydrogen carrying an extra neutron, which makes it twice as heavy as ordinary hydrogen. Kruse calls clearing it "the real game" for human health, but he argues the body is built to do this itself. The main goal, in his model, is restoring that ability; drinking water with less deuterium is a support, not the fix.

#### How the body handles deuterium

In Kruse's model, Complex IV — the last step of the mitochondrial energy chain, where oxygen is turned into water — acts as an "isotope mass gate" that makes deuterium-depleted water (DDW). Healthy mitochondria keep their internal water near 90 parts per million (ppm) of deuterium, so only light hydrogen enters ATP synthase, the cell's energy-making motor. Deuterium cannot be converted into anything else; it can only be excreted. Sunlight carries no deuterium, he notes, which is why life built its energy system on light hydrogen.

Kruse stresses that deuterium becomes a problem only after the cell's "engine" has been deformed by magnetic or electrical interference: a weak or unstable local field, nnEMF (non-native electromagnetic fields from wireless and wiring), or artificial light. When the field holding the mitochondrial membrane together collapses, deuterium floods in and jams the motor. A strong, stable horizontal field, by contrast, sorts light hydrogen from deuterium, because their nuclei respond differently to magnetism. That, he says, is why coconuts and bananas carry no penalty in El Salvador that they would in Boston. Cutting deuterium intake is always wise, in his view, but sorting it well matters more.

#### Water in nature

Light water evaporates first, so heavy water rains out early as weather moves toward the poles, and high-latitude water and food are naturally low in deuterium. Yet Kruse says northerners who live indoors under artificial light load up anyway, because a weaker shield and indoor life stop them sorting it. Warm, stagnant seas and desert surface water grow richer in deuterium as light water evaporates. Deep "primary water," which the geologist Stephan Riess held forms new deep in the earth, has never evaporated and is naturally low in deuterium; Kruse credits the Nazca with tapping deep runoff for that reason.
