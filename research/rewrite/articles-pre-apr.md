### Mitochondria as light- and field-driven electronics
- **id:** mitochondria-quantum-devices
- **pillar:** support
- **summary:** Kruse treats each mitochondrion as an antenna and electrical circuit tuned by sunlight and Earth's magnetic field, not just a chemical furnace that burns food.
- **protocols:** none

Textbooks picture mitochondria, the tiny power units inside our cells, as furnaces that burn food. Kruse argues they are better understood as electronics: antennas and circuits tuned to sunlight and to Earth's magnetic field. In his model, whether this circuit works decides almost everything else, from the state of the cell's water to how it handles oxygen, and so health or disease.

#### The Sun's receiver

Kruse links the mitochondrion to the Sun through what he calls "Optico-Magnetic Engineering." In his view both are low-energy "quantum synchrotrons" whose output depends on tunneling, the quantum trick by which a particle slips through an energy barrier it could not climb over. In the Sun, hydrogen nuclei tunnel through their mutual repulsion to fuse; in the cell, electrons taken from food tunnel between the protein complexes of the respiratory chain. He therefore sees the mitochondrion as a wireless receiver tuned to the Sun–Earth circuit, not a digestion vat.

He sums up his whole thesis as two established ideas joined together: conservation of angular momentum (spin cannot simply appear or vanish) and Rudolph Marcus's theory of how fast electrons jump between molecules. In his account this means electron flow depends on spin and on the shape of the surrounding field, not only on energy.

The link between the two is CISS, short for chiral-induced spin selectivity. Kruse explains that proteins built with one "handedness" (chirality) act as spin filters, letting through electrons that spin one way and tying each electron's forward motion to its spin. Earth's field keeps this filter aligned, an effect he calls the "Zeeman handshake." Borrowing the radical-pair idea used to explain bird navigation, he says a weak field does not force a reaction; it tips the balance between spin states, and the cell amplifies the nudge.

#### The parts as circuit components

Kruse describes the inner membrane as so thin that it carries roughly 30 million volts per meter of electric field, which he likens to a lightning bolt. Cardiolipin, a cone-shaped fat in that membrane, is the insulating sleeve that holds the respiratory complexes close together. It keeps them within 14 ångströms (1.4 billionths of a meter), the tunneling limit he credits to Dutton.

At the tips of the cristae (the membrane's inward folds), cardiolipin gathers negative charge. In his model this focuses a wave of protons the way the undersea canyon at Nazaré, Portugal, focuses ocean swells into giant waves. He calls it the "Biological Nazaré" and says it drives ATP synthase, the rotary motor that makes the cell's energy currency, toward 9,000 revolutions per minute. He also proposes that the energy packed into these tips allows "biological LENR" (low-energy nuclear reactions), such as turning copper-63 into copper-65, which he says clears isotopic waste at body temperature.

Kruse builds this electronic picture on earlier scientists. Albert Szent-Györgyi proposed in 1941 that proteins are semiconductors. John Bardeen's transistor and his theory of superconductivity, in Kruse's reading, supply the working mechanisms, and Robert O. Becker built his map of the body's direct-current healing signals on Bardeen's work. In this picture cristae act as transistors switched by neuropsin (a sensor for dawn UV-A light), and the structured water at the membrane acts as a room-temperature superconductor.

He extends the semiconductor comparison to chemistry. The chemical tags a cell adds to its proteins are, in his view, nature's controlled "doping," the deliberate impurities engineers use to tune silicon. Foreign chemicals are uncontrolled "rogue dopants" that trap electrons; he names statins, synthetic peptides and lithium.

#### Two kinds of resistance

Kruse says the circuit needs two opposite properties at once. The membrane must insulate well, like the wall of a capacitor holding charge, while the electron chain must conduct easily, like a wire. He maps this onto Martin Picard's "energy resistance" idea: health sits in a just-right zone with enough resistance to turn electron flow into useful work, but not so much that energy is wasted as heat.

Harms come in two matching kinds in his model. Some jam the wire: loss of the magnetic anchor, nnEMF (his term for man-made wireless and electrical fields), light at night, and deuterium (heavy hydrogen), heavy metals, calcium, statins and peptides acting as electron traps. Others short the capacitor: he names spike protein, lipid nanoparticles, and the heat released when the wire jams, which melts the membrane's structured water.

#### One network, ancient roots

Kruse says mitochondria are not isolated units but one wired network. The junctions between them act as a "bus bar," an electrician's term for a shared power rail, so a failure in one tissue spreads body-wide. Tissues also stay in sync wirelessly, he says, through terahertz signals that cross 20–50-nanometer gaps.

He traces this design to life's origins. Building on Lynn Margulis's account of mitochondria as once free-living bacteria, he argues they are photosynthesis machinery run in reverse. That, in his view, is why light therapy works by resonance, reminding the machinery of its original job, rather than acting like a drug.

Kruse treats the iron cores of ferritin (the body's iron-storage protein) and of the cytochromes as "ferrons." These are tiny magnetic compass needles and capacitors that, when a person is unpinned (living without a stable local field, grounding and sun), lose their bearing. They then leak iron and turn stray fields into heat.

Finally, he reads a mitochondrion's shape as a live record of its surroundings: swelling, fragmented cristae and donut shapes are the organelle reshaping itself to survive electrical, magnetic or isotopic stress, not a separate disease.

In practice, Kruse concludes that this is a hardware problem set by the environment. He says the circuit is restored by full-spectrum sunlight, grounding and a stable local magnetic field. He advises against treating a field problem with added chemicals such as statins, synthetic peptides, insulin or GLP-1 drugs, which in his model only add more electron traps.

### The field, the genes and the clock in the cell
- **id:** field-genes-telomeres
- **pillar:** support
- **summary:** In his model the environment sets the physics, the physics sets how genes behave, and DNA carries physical "mass" that light and the daily clock must keep low.
- **protocols:** none

Kruse turns the usual view of genes as the body's blueprint upside down. In his model the environment comes first: it sets the physics inside the cell, and that physics decides how genes behave. This matters to him because it means gene tests and "biological age" clocks measure late effects, while the causes lie in light, magnetism and water.

#### Environment first, genes last

Kruse reads Nature's 2024 essay arguing that genes are not the blueprint for life as mainstream biology catching up with him. He adds that it still misses the real cause: deuterium (heavy hydrogen) and the physics of the cell's water. In his view the nuclear genome is a passive library, and the slow drift of chemical marks on DNA (epigenetic drift) simply follows damage done by the field.

He stresses that DNA is paramagnetic (weakly drawn to a magnet) when wet, though not when dry. Gene activity, he says, therefore follows the strength and direction of the surrounding magnetic field, which he calls "electromagnetic terroir." In his telling, this is why ancient peoples sheltered in paramagnetic stone such as basalt and granite when the field weakened.

#### Telomeres as a record of disorder

Telomeres are the protective caps on the ends of chromosomes. Kruse adopts the TRCS (Telomere/rDNA Co-Regulation) model, from a 2026 paper he credits to Huang. It holds that the physical shortening of telomeres and of other repeated DNA stretches drives cell aging by raising p53, the protein that guards the genome. Epigenetic drift is only a passenger, which he says is why attempts to reset those marks give only temporary results.

He links this to a 2026 Shanghai study by Song and colleagues, which found a 71-terahertz vibration in mitochondria that disappears when they are dried or disrupted. Kruse reads it as a timing signal sent from mitochondria to the nucleus that paces telomeres and gene reading while the membrane holds its charge. When the cell's structured water collapses (from his healthy "160" state to ordinary "78" water), electrons slow, the signal goes quiet and the nucleus "goes blind." Telomeres, in his view, therefore record entropy (accumulated disorder), not age.

He finds support in the 2024 sequencing of the South American lungfish, whose genome is about 30 times larger than ours with roughly the same number of genes. Kruse argues genome size reflects how efficient a species' "water battery" is. A slow, low-voltage animal piles up non-coding DNA as ballast that soaks up electromagnetic noise and sheds heat. Humans get by with a lean genome because high-capacitance water and light sensors keep that battery charged. Indoor life under screens, at high latitude and under a weakening field, he says, makes humans run like the lungfish without its buffer, so telomeres shorten, men lose their Y chromosome in blood cells, and cells fall back on fermentation.

#### Time-stamping

The cell's daily clock is a loop in which the CLOCK and BMAL1 genes switch on the Per and Cry genes, whose proteins return about every 24 hours to switch themselves off. Kruse stresses that this loop also decides *when* proteins get their chemical tags (phosphate, methyl, acetyl and ubiquitin groups); he puts the share of gene products tagged on a daily rhythm at 5–10%. He calls this "time-stamping."

Indoor living and nnEMF (man-made wireless and electrical fields), he says, erase the day–night swings in temperature and magnetic field that the clock relies on. Proteins are then tagged at the wrong time, flagged as damaged and scrapped, at a heavy cost in ATP and NAD⁺ (the cell's energy currency and its main electron carrier). He calls this costly loss of stored order "Landauer liquidation" and says it pushes cells toward fermentation.

#### Mass on the base chain

Kruse borrows a Bitcoin analogy. DNA is the "base chain," and daily circadian tagging is a "layer two" that adjusts genes without rewriting the code, so the genome never stiffens. Methyl and acetyl tags, and deuterium, add literal mass to that base chain. When deuterium enters the methyl pool used to tag DNA, he says, vibrations slow, enzymes drag, the epigenetic clock stalls and the strand leaks faint light. He faults chronobiology, citing Satchin Panda's work, for treating the clock as chemical software and missing the planetary "hardware" that powers it.

He also describes a protective brake. FOXO3, a gene common in very long-lived people, can pause cell division. Kruse calls this a "clock dampener" that trades copying speed for "structural time permanence." The pause opens a window for light-driven repair, while template-free DNA-writing systems first described in bacteria (DRT3 and DRT7) rebuild telomere caps. Under LED light, nnEMF and a weakening field the opposite happens: rushed copying fractures the repeats and a p53 surge drives senescence (permanent cell retirement) or cancer.

In practice, Kruse tells people not to rely on epigenetic "Horvath" clocks, saliva telomere tests, NAD⁺ precursors or peptides, which in his model read or poke at outputs rather than causes. His rule is to avoid anything that adds "atomic mass" to the base chain; he applies it to mRNA injections and newborn vitamin K shots. Instead he says to reset the physical environment: barefoot grounding under full sun, in a place with a horizontal magnetic field.

### Information, erasure and the chromosome 2 "safe mode"
- **id:** landauer-erasure
- **pillar:** support
- **summary:** Kruse frames disease as lost information rather than low energy: corrupted proteins and falling voltage are "erased" at a cost in heat, and under enough stress a fuse on chromosome 2 drops the body into a primitive safe mode.
- **protocols:** none

Kruse argues that disease is, at root, a loss of information rather than a shortage of energy. Damaged proteins and a falling cell voltage are "erased," and every erasure costs heat. Under extreme stress, he says, a built-in fuse on chromosome 2 trips and drops the body into a primitive "safe mode." In his model this explains why many modern illnesses look like the body deliberately downgrading itself.

#### Information matters more than energy

In Kruse's view, the precision of the cell's spin signals matters more than the amount of energy it has, because energy without direction is only heat. Blue light, nnEMF (man-made wireless and electrical fields) and a weakening magnetic field harm cells mainly by turning their data into noise, not by cutting ATP output. On this point he says mitochondrial researchers Doug Wallace and Nick Lane miss what matters.

#### Erasure and its heat

He builds on physicist Rolf Landauer, who showed in 1961 that erasing one bit of information must release a minimum amount of heat. Kruse treats a well-folded protein as a clean channel through which electrons pass with almost no waste. When the cell's structured water degrades and magnetic pinning is lost, the protein's bandgap (the energy step that lets it act as a semiconductor) narrows, electrons stall and the protein misfolds. It has become, in his words, a "corrupt bit."

The cell's answer is ubiquitin tagging. Enzymes spot the protein's lost native field, mark it with ubiquitin (a small "delete" label), and the proteasome, the cell's shredder, unwinds and cuts it. Kruse calls this Landauer erasure in molecular form.

He applies the same logic to voltage. A cell's resting charge is, for him, its information capacity, so the slide from a healthy −70 millivolts toward a fermenting −15 millivolts counts as information being erased. Each erasure dumps heat, the heat dries out structured water, and drier water corrupts more proteins. The cell is trapped in a failure-and-clean-up loop that bankrupts its ATP and NAD⁺ (its main electron carrier). Summed across a tissue, he says, this heat is what people feel as a hot flash.

#### Repair before erasure

Kruse says the cell first tries a local repair, which he calls the "ubiquitin–G3P pivot." Ubiquitin brings glucose to the misfolded protein, and the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle there supplies deuterium-poor electrons that let it refold.

The unfolded-protein response, the alarm raised when proteins fold badly, is his "error log." Its three sensors, PERK, ATF6 and IRE1, act in his account as antennas that go out of tune as water thickens. PERK halts protein production so less heat is made, and the protein-building compartment expands to give melanin and heme more surface for a magnetic shield. If the deuterium load or field loss is too great, repair gives way to cell death, which he marks as a final red-shifted burst of faint light.

#### The chromosome 2 fuse

Human chromosome 2 formed when two ancestral ape chromosomes fused. A leftover stretch of telomere sequence (chromosome-end cap) remains inside it at the fusion point, and Kruse treats it as a field-sensitive fuse. Under the TRCS model he follows (Telomere/rDNA Co-Regulation), when a voltage crash threatens to erase the genome, this loop "blows." Triggers he names include cosmic rays and stray light during field loss, poor sleep, very low cholesterol and deuterium-heavy food out of season.

A surge of p53, the genome's guardian protein, then forces what he calls an "atavistic retreat": tissues revert to older, simpler designs, like a satellite powering down to safe mode as it crosses the South Atlantic Anomaly. Nerve, myelin and pigment tissue shrink so the body becomes a "worse antenna" for electromagnetic noise. The cell also stops spending energy on rigid collagen, loosening connective tissue on purpose to buy time. The diaphragm and lower esophageal sphincter slip back to a looser template. In his account this is the shared cause of hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, reflux, hiatal hernia and sleep apnea. It is also why he says repeated ligament injections fail: the voltage problem is never fixed.

The fusion site, he says, sits among four genes that shaped modern humans, which he calls a "quantum blockchain." POMC makes the hormone that drives melanin. EDAR builds the sweat glands that cool the body. GCG makes glucagon and the gut hormones that run the pancreas's bicarbonate flush, which he calls the gut's isotope exhaust. BCL11A switches blood from fetal to adult hemoglobin, and in the safe-mode surge, he says, blood shifts back toward the fetal form to fuel repair.

#### Sunlight and the p53 balance

Kruse adds that whole-spectrum sunlight activates p53, which promotes DNA repair. That stops stray chromatin fragments (loose bits of DNA and protein) from piling up outside the nucleus. Left to accumulate, he says, they trip the cGAS-STING alarm and the inflammatory signaling of aged cells, opening the door to chronic disease and frailty. In his view this light-driven switch is the missing explanation for why drugs that clear aged cells have so far disappointed.

In practice, Kruse reads safe-mode symptoms as a message to change the environment that caused them, not to suppress them. To keep DNA damage low and p53 working, he points to whole-spectrum sunlight, avoiding toxins and strong magnetic pinning (living under a stable local field while grounded and sunlit). For older people facing a weakening field, he says higher cholesterol helps hold the membrane's charge and avoid the safe-mode shutdown.

### Water: the "160 vs 78" battery
- **id:** water-160-vs-78
- **pillar:** water
- **summary:** Kruse says healthy mitochondrial water is structured, with a dielectric he numbers at 160, and disease collapses it to ordinary bulk water at 78.
- **protocols:** none

This is the hinge of most of Kruse's teaching. He says the water lining the inner mitochondrial membrane is not ordinary water but a structured layer that stores a great deal of electric charge. He describes its state with a single number, a dielectric constant of 160, and says disease collapses it to ordinary "bulk" water at 78. Nearly every illness in his model passes through this collapse.

#### What the numbers mean

A dielectric constant measures how much electric charge a substance can hold and how strongly it buffers electric fields. Kruse treats the structured water at the membrane as a battery, or capacitor, with a high value of 160, and ordinary water as a much weaker one at 78.

He identifies the structured state with Gerald Pollack's exclusion-zone (EZ) water, an ordered layer that forms against surfaces and pushes dissolved particles out. In his model dawn UV-A light and near-infrared light build this layer, and a horizontal magnetic field anchors it so it does not discharge.

Following Gilbert Ling, Kruse says potassium anchors this "water table," with melanin as its partner; he calls the two "brothers." Disturbed potassium handling is therefore the first sign of breakdown. He blames potassium-wasting diuretics such as Lasix for collapsing the structure. He describes Ling's contribution as finding the battery's container, and his own as identifying the fuel: light and magnetic fields driving electron flow.

#### Water's two spin forms

Kruse also explains the two states through spin. Ordinary water is a fixed 3-to-1 mix of two forms: ortho-water, whose hydrogen nuclei spin in step, and para-water, whose spins cancel. He calls ortho-water the "grease": magnetic, sticky and viscous, the 78 state where deuterium (heavy hydrogen) builds up. Para-water, with no spin noise, packs into the ordered 160 layer that lets protons pass without friction; he calls it the "superconductor."

Carbon dioxide tips the balance in his account. When the body holds on to CO₂ and local tissue pH sits around 6.8–7.1, he says water shifts toward the para form. Hemoglobin senses that change and releases oxygen gently, in its safe form.

#### What the collapse does

When water falls from 160 to 78, Kruse says electron and proton transfer slow about 1.43-fold. That pushes the respiratory chain into "Marcus inversion," his name for the stalled state in which pushing harder moves fewer electrons. He reports his own computer model of the spin filter that guides electrons (CISS): it runs at about 32% efficiency in 160 water but under 9% in 78 water, a loss he calls the point where the cell loses its identity.

Stalled electrons release heat, which melts more structure and thickens spinal fluid. In a full meltdown the membrane's charge swings positive and the cell leaks UPE, the faint light given off by stressed cells. He describes the result as tissue turning into a cloudy lens that scatters light, and says this pushes heteroplasmy (the share of damaged mitochondrial DNA) past its tipping point.

Structured water is also, in Kruse's account, an isotope shield. At 160 it acts as a mass filter that keeps deuterium away from the nucleus and DNA; at 78 heavy isotopes get in and load NAD⁺, the cell's main electron carrier, with deuterium.

He lists everyday causes of the collapse. Isolated blue light from screens and LEDs lacks the red and infrared needed to rebuild structure. nnEMF (man-made wireless and electrical fields) and loss of magnetic pinning do similar harm. So, he says, do petroleum and silicone lotions applied to skin after a warm shower, which he says halve the skin's charge-holding capacity much as blue light does.

#### Spirals and the heart

Kruse says spirals order water. His rule of thumb is "three turns sense, seven turns resonate," meaning a seven-turn spiral pushes nearby water toward the 160 state. He says the heart's helical chambers spin blood this way to control its deuterium and thickness, keyed by the vagus nerve.

In practice, Kruse's route back to 160 is environmental. He points to morning sun (UV-A plus infrared), grounding and magnetic pinning (living under a stable local field while grounded and sunlit), together with restoring potassium and avoiding drugs that waste it. He sees two other paths to the same result: the bicarbonate loop built from carbon dioxide, and dietary DHA. He also suggests drinking water from deep volcanic fissures ("primary water") and skipping petroleum or silicone lotions after showering.

### Oxygen's spin and the paramagnetic gate
- **id:** magnetic-excursion-oxygen
- **pillar:** magnetism
- **summary:** In his model Earth's field keeps oxygen in its safe triplet state at the end of the electron chain, and a weakening field flips it to reactive singlet oxygen.
- **protocols:** pin-first-order

Oxygen can exist in two spin states, and Kruse makes the difference between them a master switch for health. In his model Earth's magnetic field keeps oxygen in its calm "triplet" form where cells use it. A weakening field, or man-made fields and light, flips it into reactive "singlet" oxygen that burns tissue.

#### Why ordinary oxygen is safe

The oxygen we breathe is triplet oxygen: its two outer electrons spin in the same direction. That makes it paramagnetic (weakly drawn to a magnet). Kruse stresses that the molecules of our tissues have paired, opposite spins, so oxygen cannot react with them directly without breaking the rule that spin is conserved. This "spin barrier," he says, is what makes air-breathing life possible. Oxygen only reacts when a precise catalyst realigns the spins, which is exactly what the cell's electron chain does.

Singlet oxygen is the same molecule with its spins paired. In his account it is diamagnetic (weakly pushed away by a magnet), does no useful work and reacts violently.

#### The paramagnetic gate

At the end of the electron chain sits cytochrome c oxidase, or Complex IV. Kruse calls its copper–iron center a "paramagnetic gate," a checkpoint that matches incoming electrons to oxygen's spin with the help of Earth's field. When the gate works, spin-aligned electrons tunnel in smoothly, oxygen becomes deuterium-depleted water (water low in heavy hydrogen), and the membrane holds about −70 millivolts. He says the center must use the heavy copper isotope copper-65, citing Lahoud and Moynier's work on brain copper isotopes. He adds that paramagnetic oxygen is drawn toward the small magnetic fields of the spinning ATP synthase motors.

He cites a 2025 study by Beutner and colleagues as support for the field's role. In it, isolated heart mitochondria placed in a weak static magnetic field, far weaker than a fridge magnet, raised their oxygen use by about 40% while making fewer damaging oxygen by-products, but only within a narrow sweet spot. He reads this as the field keeping electron spins parallel. Melanin, DHA and cholesterol, he says, help a strong field hold oxygen's spins in place, and grounding supplies free electrons that do the same.

#### The planet's oxygen and its field

Kruse reads a 2025 Science Advances paper, which tied Earth's oxygen levels to the strength of its magnetic dipole over 540 million years, as proof that the planet's core is the master and oxygen the servant. In his telling, ocean currents coupled to the field after oxygen first built up in the atmosphere. That locked oxygen in its triplet state and made the Cambrian explosion of complex animals possible.

#### When the gate fails

Because magnetism controls spin, Kruse argues, a weakening core field is now pushing oxygen toward singlet in many zip codes and "liquidating" tissue. nnEMF (man-made wireless and electrical fields), isolated blue light and deuterium do the same locally; the heavier bond deuterium forms with oxygen, he says, resists normal triplet chemistry. Once oxygen flips, it attacks cardiolipin (the membrane's insulating fat), mitochondrial DNA and iron–sulfur clusters. Voltage falls toward −15 millivolts and production of clean water stops.

Citing what he calls a European Space Agency report that the inner core has reversed its rotation, he describes field chaos driving cells toward fermentation by three routes. Electron pairs stay in the wrong spin state too long, flipping oxygen to singlet. Iron "compass" cores in cells lose their north and soak up stray fields. And cell water loses its charge-holding structure.

#### What singlet oxygen destroys

Kruse lists the damage. Collagen needs triplet oxygen for the enzyme that builds hydroxyproline, one of its key amino acids. When oxygen goes singlet, he says, collagen production stalls and tissues loosen, an "environmental" form of Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. Melanin, tooth enamel and fats burn, and heme proteins, including the liver's CYP enzymes and hormone machinery, are lost. He calls singlet oxygen a "thermal hot potato" that cooks retina, skin, hair and mitochondria.

It also corrupts the cell's light. In Kruse's model triplet oxygen releases energy quietly, with very little stray light. Singlet oxygen sets off chain reactions in fats whose decay throws out disordered bursts of UPE (faint light from cells). That noise drowns the coherent UV signals he says guide cell division.

Not every oxygen by-product is bad in his account. Brief superoxide pulses act as healthy signals, like Morse code between the nucleus and mitochondria, and he says diabetics have exhausted them.

In practice, Kruse's answer is the "pin first" order: fix your magnetic and wireless environment first, add morning light second, and change exercise or diet only after that. He also warns that moving to a different magnetic field flips oxygen at once and costs the vagus nerve its ability to clear deuterium, so a move should be deliberate. To stand in for a weakening field, he suggests sleeping on a north-seeking static magnetic pad, taking pure DHA and getting raw morning UV-A sunlight.

### Marcus inversion and the Warburg shift
- **id:** marcus-theory-inverted-region
- **pillar:** light
- **summary:** Kruse's core failure state: electron transfer stalls in Marcus's "inverted region," and cells retreat to fermentation as a protective emergency mode.
- **protocols:** none

Kruse's central picture of how cells fail comes from chemistry. Electron transfer, he argues, can stall in a state where pushing harder makes things worse, the "inverted region" described by Rudolph Marcus. Cells caught there retreat to fermentation as an emergency mode. In his model this one sequence underlies cancer, faster aging and neurodegeneration.

#### The inverted region

Marcus's theory, which won him the 1992 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, describes how fast an electron jumps from one molecule to another. Up to a point, a bigger driving force (the energy pushing the electron) makes the jump faster. Past a peak, set by the energy the surroundings must spend rearranging themselves, the rate falls again. That falling side is the inverted region. Kruse likens it to an electronic tunnel diode, where raising the voltage lowers the current. That is why, he says, a stuck chain cannot be fixed by pushing harder.

He places health at the top of this curve, matched with Martin Picard's "just-right" zone of energy resistance. Distance matters too: in his model, pushing the respiratory complexes just one ångström (a ten-billionth of a meter) farther apart cuts tunneling about tenfold.

#### What pushes cells there

Kruse names field loss, nnEMF (man-made wireless and electrical fields), blue light and calcium overload as the main pushes. Deuterium (heavy hydrogen) adds its own: with twice hydrogen's mass, he says, its spin precesses (wobbles) out of step with the chain's spin filter, causing backscatter and friction.
